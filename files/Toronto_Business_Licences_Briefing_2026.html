About this briefing

This data briefing analyses 160,248 business licence records published by the City of Toronto’s Municipal Licensing & Standards division on Toronto Open Data (extract dated 2026-11-14). It asks whether licence issuance has grown steadily or in distinct phases, which years stand out as shocks, and whether any event permanently changed the level of issuance. The same questions are then asked for seven groups of licence categories. The methods are explained in plain language in Appendix A; detailed statistical results are in Appendix B.

Key findings

  • Toronto business licence issuance has moved through five distinct growth phases since 1946. The changes in pace are statistically confirmed by every test applied, not visual impressions.
  • Growth was fastest in 1952–1963 (about 37 per cent a year, from a very small base), steady at about 7.6 per cent a year in 1964–1991, and accelerated to about 17.3 per cent a year in 1992–2006, a period that includes the 1998 amalgamation.
  • Since about 2007, issuance has been on a plateau. Excluding the pandemic years, the City has issued between 5,226 and 6,971 licences a year, with no significant upward or downward trend.
  • COVID-19 caused the largest modern shock. Issuance fell about 49 per cent below the expected level in 2020 and 41 per cent below in 2021, then returned to the pre-pandemic plateau in 2022.
  • No permanent jump or drop in the overall level was found in any year. The big changes in this series are changes in the pace of growth, not one-time shifts.
  • The unusually high count in 1957 is a data artefact: almost all of that year’s records carry a placeholder date of January 1, 1957.
  • Sectors tell different stories. The post-2006 plateau appears in almost every group of licences. But while food and drink and shops and services fully recovered from COVID-19, personal care and trades and contractors remain below their pre-pandemic path, and trades and contractors has been declining by about 9 per cent a year since 2014.
  • Early decades should be read with care: records before about 1990 most likely reflect licences that are still on file, not every licence originally issued.

Reading eight decades of licence data

Business licences are one of the few administrative records that track Toronto’s commercial activity over many decades.

Every restaurant, taxi, renovator, personal-services setting and many other businesses need a municipal licence. The number of licences issued each year therefore offers a long view of how the City’s regulated business activity has evolved, through decades of economic growth, the 1998 amalgamation of the former municipalities, changes in record-keeping, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

A chart of the raw counts suggests several growth phases and a sharp dip in 2020–21. But visual impressions can mislead: a bend in a line can be random noise, and a dip can be part of a longer trend. This briefing tests these patterns formally. It examines:

  • whether licence issuance grew at one steady pace or moved through distinct phases, and if so, when the pace changed;
  • whether particular years show temporary shocks that quickly faded;
  • whether any event caused a permanent shift in the level of issuance;
  • whether these patterns differ across seven groups of licence categories.

The four patterns we look for are illustrated in Chart 1.

Eight decades, five growth phases

Licence issuance did not grow at a single, steady pace. The statistical model that best fits the data divides the period since 1946 into five phases, separated by four turning points around 1952, 1964, 1991, 2007. (See Chart 2.)

The turning points were found by the data, not chosen by eye. A computer search tried every possible location and number of turning points, and a standard model-selection score picked four as the best balance between fitting the data and keeping the model simple. Each change in pace then passed four separate statistical tests. (See Appendix A.)

  • 1946–1951: a handful of licences a year (0 to 9). These are most likely old licences still on file.
  • 1952–1963: very rapid growth from a tiny base.
  • 1964–1991: long, steady growth of about 7.6 per cent a year, the most stable phase.
  • 1992–2006: growth more than doubled, to about 17.3 per cent a year. This period includes the 1998 amalgamation and the harmonisation of licensing rules that followed.
  • 2007–2025: a plateau, with no significant growth.

Each phase is a straight line on a log scale

Chart 2 shows the counts as they are, which makes the early decades look flat and lets the recent years dominate. On a logarithmic scale (Chart 3), equal vertical distances mean equal percentage changes, so a period of steady growth appears as a straight line, whatever the size of the count. Each of the five phases becomes a straight segment, and the steepness of the segment is the annual growth rate printed beside it. The bends are the four structural breaks; the shaded band around each break is its 95 per cent confidence range. On this scale the one-off shocks also stand out as points far from the line: the 1948 and 1957 spikes, the 1968–69 dip, and the 2020–21 COVID-19 drop.

The most important turning point for today’s planning is the most recent one. Around 2007 (95 per cent confidence range: 2003.8 to 2009.6), the fast growth of the previous 15 years stopped. Since then the number of licences issued each year has moved up and down within a narrow band, without a trend. (See Chart 4.)

This is the most precisely dated of the four turning points, and it is not caused by a single sector: as later sections show, almost every group of licences levelled off at about the same time.

A plateau in licences issued does not necessarily mean that Toronto’s economy stopped growing. It means that the number of new and renewed licences recorded in this dataset each year stopped growing. Changes in which businesses need licences, in renewal practices, and in how records are kept can all influence this count.

COVID-19: a deep but temporary drop

The pandemic produced the largest departure from trend in the modern series. In 2020 the City issued 3,244 licences, about 49 per cent below the level expected from the pre-pandemic plateau. In 2021 it issued 3,753, about 41 per cent below. (See Chart 5.)

The drop did not last. In 2022 issuance returned to 6,106, and 2023–2025 sit on the pre-2020 plateau.

To test this formally, we compared six possible explanations of the pandemic years, from “no effect” to “a permanent change in level and growth”. The best-supported explanation by far is a temporary two-year dip. A permanent level shift starting in 2020 is not supported (p = 0.101). Once the dip is accounted for, there is no evidence of any lasting change after 2022 (p ≈ 1.00).

The partial data for 2026 support this conclusion. By 2026-11-14, 5,506 licences had been issued, which is about 6,320 on a full-year basis, close to the model’s projection of about 6,521.

With only four post-pandemic years, a small lasting effect cannot be ruled out. But in the total, there is no sign of one.

Changes in pace, not one-time jumps

To find shocks without assuming when they happened, we tested every year from 1947 to 2025. For each year, the model was asked: does the fit improve if this year gets its own one-off adjustment (a temporary shock), or if the level shifts up or down from this year onward (a permanent shift)? (See Chart 6.)

Testing 80 years at once creates a trap: by chance alone, a few years will always look unusual. To avoid false alarms, the bar for “significant” was set by simulation. We generated 299 artificial histories from a model with no shocks, ran the same 80-year search on each, and recorded how unusual the most extreme year looked. A real shock had to beat 95 per cent of these chance results.

Only a few years cleared the bar:

  • COVID-19 (2020–21): the only significant shock in the modern series;
  • 1957: a large spike that is a data artefact (see box);
  • 1948 and 1968–69: small departures in years with very few records.

No year showed a permanent shift in level. The large changes in this series are changes in the pace of growth, which the five-phase trend already captures.

A note on 1957

The year 1957 has 108 licences, about 13 times the trend. 103 of them are dated exactly January 1, 1957, a placeholder that usually means only the year was known when old records were moved into a new system. A direct test confirms that 1957 is a one-year spike, not a permanent change. It is treated as an artefact throughout this briefing.

Sectors tell different stories

The overall plateau is shared across sectors, but the pandemic’s legacy is not.

The total combines very different businesses. To see whether the pattern holds across sectors, we grouped the 91 licence categories into seven groups and repeated the analysis for each. (See Chart 7.)

A shared plateau. Food and drink, personal care, vehicles and transport, entertainment, and shops and services all show a turning point between about 2004 and 2010, after which growth stopped or slowed sharply. The overall plateau is city-wide, not the result of one sector.

Hidden level shifts. Some groups show permanent shifts that are too small to register in the total:

  • Food and drink: upward shifts in 1976 and 2000 (the second soon after amalgamation);
  • Personal care: an upward shift in 2014, which probably reflects a change in which businesses had to be licensed;
  • Permits and signs: a downward shift in 2017, relative to a steep long-run trend.

One sector in decline. Trades and contractors is the only group with a clear downward trend, falling about 9 per cent a year since 2014.

The pandemic hit every sector, but recovery varied (see Chart 9):

  • Full recovery: food and drink, and shops and services, returned to their pre-pandemic paths.
  • Incomplete recovery: personal care (about 24 per cent below its pre-2020 path) and trades and contractors (about 27 per cent below, on top of a falling trend).
  • Possible lasting effect: vehicles and transport remains below its 2016–19 level, but the evidence is borderline (p = 0.067).
  • Above trend: entertainment issued more licences after 2022 than its trend implies, including a sharp rise in 2025. Counts are small, so this needs confirming with the full 2026 data.

In the total, these movements largely cancel out, which is why the overall series looks fully recovered.

What the findings mean

This briefing shows that the history of Toronto business licence issuance is best described as a sequence of growth phases, separated by statistically confirmed changes in pace, rather than as a smooth curve or a series of one-off jumps. The most recent phase, which began around 2007, is a plateau.

For anyone using these data to plan, forecast, or evaluate policy, three implications stand out.

Use the plateau, not the long-run average, as the baseline. Averaging growth across the whole period, or projecting the 1990s pace forward, would greatly overstate expected issuance. Since 2007 the best description is a stable level of roughly 6,200 licences a year.

Treat 2020–21 as a temporary disruption. For the total, the pandemic years are best modelled as a one-off dip. Including them as ordinary years would bend estimated trends downward.

Look beneath the total. The overall recovery hides sectors that remain below their pre-pandemic path. Personal care, and especially trades and contractors, warrant closer monitoring. The decline in trades and contractors began before the pandemic, which suggests longer-term causes such as regulatory change, consolidation, or shifts in the renovation market.

Strengthening the data

Several findings are limited by how historical records are stored. The value of these data for long-run analysis could be improved by:

  • flagging migrated records whose issue date is a placeholder (such as January 1) rather than a true date;
  • retaining records of expired and cancelled licences, so that early years reflect all licences issued, not only those still on file;
  • documenting changes in licence categories (for example, when a category was introduced, merged, or redefined), so that breaks caused by regulation can be told apart from changes in business activity.

Limitations

The extract is not a complete historical register: before about 1990, counts most likely reflect surviving records. Results for small groups (entertainment, and permits and signs) are less certain. The category results also depend on how the categories were grouped. Finally, the analysis describes when the pace changed; it does not, by itself, establish why.

Appendix A

Methodology

About the research

This briefing examines how the number of business licences issued in Toronto has changed since 1946. It asks four questions:

  1. Did issuance grow at one steady pace, or in distinct phases? If in phases, when did the pace change, and are those changes statistically real?
  2. Which years, if any, show temporary shocks that faded quickly?
  3. Did any event cause a permanent shift in the level of issuance?
  4. Do these patterns differ across groups of licence categories?

Data

We used the Business Licences and Permits dataset published by the City of Toronto’s Municipal Licensing & Standards division on Toronto Open Data. The extract, dated 2026-11-14, contains 160,248 licence records. We counted the number of licences issued in each calendar year using the Issued date.

The analysis uses the 80 complete years from 1946 to 2025 (154,715 licences). The year 2026 was incomplete at the time of the extract, so it was used only to check the model’s predictions.

Data caveats

Surviving records. The dataset holds licence records as they exist today. Years before about 1990 most likely contain only licences that are still on file, so early growth partly reflects which old records survived.

Placeholder dates. Many records from 1957 and from 1961–1972, and some from 2004, are dated January 1. This usually means only the year was known when records were moved between systems. These dates create artificial spikes, which the analysis treats as artefacts.

Large swings from year to year. Annual counts vary far more than simple chance would predict. In technical terms, they are overdispersed: their variance is about 125 times what a basic (Poisson) count model assumes. We therefore used a negative-binomial model, a standard model for counts that allows for this extra variation. Without it, almost every year would wrongly look like a significant shock.

Why a log scale?

Licence counts range from zero to almost 7,000. On an ordinary scale, early decades look flat and every recent wobble looks huge. On a logarithmic (log) scale, a constant percentage growth rate appears as a straight line, whether the count is 10 or 10,000. So on a log scale, a “phase” is simply a straight stretch of line, and a “structural break” is a point where the line bends. All slopes in this briefing are therefore annual growth rates in per cent.

Step 1: Finding the growth phases

We used joinpoint regression (also called segmented regression). Imagine laying a flexible ruler along the points of a log-scale chart. The ruler is straight between a few “joints”, where it can bend. The computer tries every possible location for the joints and chooses the locations that bring the ruler closest to the data. Each straight section is a growth phase; each joint is a turning point, or structural break.

Two choices keep this honest:

  • The years 1957, 2020, and 2021 get their own one-off adjustments, so that one spike and the pandemic dip cannot bend the long-run line.
  • The computer, not the analyst, chooses where the joints go. The method also reports a confidence range for each turning point.

Step 2: Choosing the number of phases

More joints always fit the data a little better, so “best fit” alone would add joints forever. We used the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), a score that rewards a close fit but charges a penalty for every extra joint. The model with the lowest score wins. BIC was lowest with four turning points (five phases); a fifth or sixth turning point made the score worse. (See Chart A1.)

Step 3: Testing whether each change in pace is real

A turning point chosen by the computer could still be a fluke. Each of the four changes in pace was therefore tested four ways, and kept only if it passed all four:

  1. A standard test of whether the slope changes at that point.
  2. The same test with robust standard errors (Newey–West), which stay valid if neighbouring years are similar to each other or some years are noisier than others.
  3. A count-model test using the negative-binomial model on the original counts rather than the log scale.
  4. A “remove-the-joint” test: delete one turning point, let the computer re-fit the others, and check whether the fit gets significantly worse. This accounts for the fact that the dates were chosen by searching.

All four changes in pace passed every test at the 0.1 per cent level.

Step 4: Checking the turning points another way

Joinpoint regression forces the line to be continuous: it can bend, but not jump. To check that the turning points were not an artefact of that choice, we also used methods that make different assumptions:

  • Bai–Perron multiple-break regression, which lets the line jump as well as bend at each break;
  • sup-F, ave-F, and exp-F tests, which test “no break at all” against “a break at some unknown date”;
  • CUSUM and MOSUM tests, which track whether a single straight trend’s prediction errors drift away over time;
  • Chow tests at candidate years.

Every one of these tests rejected the idea of a single steady trend. Bai–Perron placed its breaks in the early 1950s, mid-1960s, and early-to-mid 2000s, in line with the joinpoint results. Its estimate of a 1980s–1990s break was less precise, so that turning point’s date is the least certain of the four.

Step 5: Searching every year for shocks

To find shocks without assuming when they occurred, we used a forward search on the negative-binomial count model with the five-phase trend:

  • For every year, we added one extra term and measured how much the fit improved (the likelihood-ratio statistic). We tried three kinds of term: a one-year shock, a two-year shock, and a permanent step from that year onward.
  • The year with the largest improvement was tested for significance. If significant, it was added to the model and the search was repeated, until no further year was significant.
  • Permanent steps were searched only after significant one-year shocks were in the model. Otherwise, a single spike could be mistaken for “a step up followed by a step down”.

Why simulation is needed: the 80-coin problem

Flip 80 coins five times each, and a few will come up heads every time by pure chance. Testing 80 years for a shock creates the same problem: some years will look unusual even when nothing happened.

An ordinary p-value ignores this and produces false alarms. A simple correction (Bonferroni) over-corrects, because neighbouring years are not independent.

We used a parametric bootstrap instead. The model without any shock was used to simulate 299 artificial 80-year histories. The same search was run on each, recording the most extreme result. A real shock had to be stronger than 95 per cent of these “most extreme by chance” results. This sets a fair bar that allows for the search.

Step 6: Testing known dates directly

When the date of a possible event is known in advance, no search is needed and standard tests apply. We tested two events directly:

  • 1957: is it a one-year spike, a permanent step, or both?
  • COVID-19: we compared six explanations, from no effect to a temporary dip, a permanent level shift, a change in level and growth, and a dip followed by a lasting change from 2022. Models were compared using the Akaike and Bayesian Information Criteria (AIC and BIC) and likelihood-ratio tests. This is the interrupted-time-series approach widely used to evaluate policy changes and public health events.

Step 7: Checking the assumptions

Each test relies on assumptions that were checked directly:

  • Extra variation: the final negative-binomial model’s dispersion is 1.26, close to the ideal value of 1.
  • Year-to-year dependence: once the phases are modelled, there is little correlation left between neighbouring years (Breusch–Godfrey p = 0.221; Durbin–Watson p = 0.641). The simulation-based tests assume this, so it matters.
  • Unit-root tests (ADF, Phillips–Perron, KPSS) suggest a “random walk” when breaks are ignored. This is a well-known side effect of unmodelled structural breaks, and is consistent with the phases found.
  • Uneven noise: the log-scale residuals are non-normal and noisier in early years, which is why every change in pace was also tested with robust errors and in the count model. Conclusions did not change.

Step 8: Analysis by category group

The total combines very different businesses, so the analysis was repeated for seven groups. Each of the 91 licence categories was assigned to a group by keyword. (See Table A1.)

For each group:

  • the sample starts at the first year with at least one licence and an average of at least three a year over that year and the next two, to avoid long runs of zeros;
  • the number of phases (zero to four) was chosen by BIC, and every phase had to last at least 6 years, so a short burst could not count as a phase;
  • the shock search used 199 simulated histories per step, and the six COVID-19 explanations were compared as for the total.

Software

All analysis was done in R 4.4.1 using the packages MASS, segmented, strucchange, tsoutliers, urca, tseries, lmtest, and sandwich. Scripts: analysis.R (total series) and category_analysis.R (category groups).

Why these methods, and not others

Several common approaches were considered and not used as the main method.

Approach Why it was not the main method
Choosing turning points by eye Choosing dates from a chart and then testing them on the same data overstates significance. Here the computer chose the dates, and the tests account for the search.
A single break test (Chow, Zivot–Andrews) These allow only one break; the data show at least four. They were used only as supporting checks.
One straight trend or a smooth curve Every stability test rejected a single trend. A smooth curve gives no turning points and no growth rate per phase.
Smoothing filters (moving averages, LOESS, Hodrick–Prescott) They draw a smooth line but give no break dates and no significance tests, and they smear a sharp shock such as COVID-19 across neighbouring years.
Change-point detection on growth rates (PELT) Year-on-year growth is very noisy when early counts are tiny, and this method gives no confidence ranges or p-values. It was run only as an exploratory check.
A time-series (ARIMA) model alone ARIMA treats changes in pace as random drift, so it cannot say where the pace changed. It was used only to cross-check the shocks.
Markov-switching models These assume the series moves back and forth between recurring states. Here the phases happen once, in sequence, and 80 years of data is too short.
A basic (Poisson) count model Annual counts vary about 125 times more than it allows, so almost every year would look like a shock.
An ordinary p-value for each year Testing 80 years produces false alarms by chance. Simulation sets a fair bar that allows for the search.

In short, the chosen combination lets the data choose when the pace changed and how many phases there are, tests each change after allowing for the search, respects the count nature of the data, and separates changes in pace from temporary shocks and permanent shifts.

Reading the category results

  • Food and drink (70,082 licences): four phases; plateau since 2006; upward level shifts in 1976 and 2000; full COVID-19 recovery.
  • Personal care: three phases; upward level shift in 2014; incomplete recovery after COVID-19.
  • Trades and contractors: growth slowed after the mid-1970s and turned negative after about 2014; deepest lasting COVID-19 effect.
  • Vehicles and transport: plateau since 2004; contains most of the 1957 artefact; borderline evidence of a lasting post-pandemic shortfall.

 

  • Entertainment: small counts; plateau since 2006; the largest proportional drop in 2020, and above trend since 2022.
  • Permits and signs: a single steep trend since 1998, with no significant turning point; very noisy.
  • Shops and services: plateau since about 2010; no significant level shifts; full COVID-19 recovery.
