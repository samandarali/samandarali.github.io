Introduction to Commercial Credit Analysis

⌄

When a business requests credit, the lender must determine whether the loan can be repaid as agreed - not simply whether the business is profitable or owns valuable assets. Commercial credit analysis provides a structured process for making that determination.

What this page covers

This page introduces the main stages of commercial credit analysis. We begin by examining the four principal financial statements and using a real-company example to understand what each statement reveals about financial performance, financial position, and cash flow. We then calculate and interpret key ratios used to assess profitability, liquidity, leverage, debt-service coverage, and operating efficiency.

Building on this financial foundation, we bring the findings together in a complete credit assessment. This includes understanding the borrower and the purpose of the financing, evaluating repayment capacity, identifying financial and qualitative risks, reviewing projections and collateral, assessing the proposed loan structure, and forming an evidence-based credit recommendation. Finally, a practical credit case provides an opportunity to apply the complete process.

Understand the Financial Statements

⌄

Financial statements are formal accounting reports that provide information about a company's financial position, financial performance, cash flows, and changes in equity.

Four Main Financial Statements

⌄

The four main financial statements are the Balance Sheet, Income Statement, Cash Flow Statement, and Statement of Changes in Equity. Below, we examine what each statement shows and why it matters in commercial credit analysis.

Balance Sheet

Shows the company's financial position at a specific point in time - what it owns, what it owes, and the owners' residual interest.

Key information
Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity
Credit question:
How liquid, leveraged, and financially strong is the borrower?

Cash Flow Statement

Shows the company's actual cash inflows and outflows during a period.

Key information
Operating activities
Investing activities
Financing activities
Credit question:
Does the business actually generate enough cash to service its debt?

Important: A profitable company can still experience repayment problems if its operations do not generate sufficient cash.

Income Statement

Shows the company's financial performance over a period and whether its operations generate profit.

Key information
Revenue → COGS → Gross Profit → Operating Expenses → EBIT → Interest → Taxes → Net Income
Credit question:
Is the borrower generating sufficient and sustainable earnings?

Statement of Changes in Equity

Explains how shareholders’ equity changed during the period.

Key information
Net income · Dividends · Share issuance · Share repurchases · Other comprehensive income
Credit question:
What is causing the owners’ financial interest in the business to increase or decrease?

Why lenders analyze the statements together

For credit analysis, financial statements should not be analyzed independently. The income statement shows whether the borrower is profitable, the balance sheet shows its financial position and leverage, and the cash flow statement shows whether the borrower actually generates enough cash to service its obligations. The statement of changes in equity helps explain how profits, dividends, share transactions, and other changes affect the owners' financial interest in the business.

Analyze and Interpret Financial Ratios

⌄

Financial Ratio Analysis

⌄

Ratios should rarely be interpreted in isolation. A commercial credit analyst normally compares them with the borrower's historical performance, industry averages, competitors, lending covenants, and the characteristics of the business.

Liquidity

⌄

Liquidity measures the borrower's ability to meet short-term obligations when they become due.

Current Ratio

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Current Assets = $147,957M

Current Liabilities = $165,631M

Current Ratio = 147,957 ÷ 165,631
= 0.89×

Interpretation: Apple had $0.89 of current assets for every $1.00 of current liabilities in FY2025. This indicates negative working capital and suggests that Apple depends partly on continuing operating cash inflows and access to financing to meet its short-term obligations. A lender should assess this result alongside Apple’s historical trend, industry norms, operating cash flow, and liquidity forecast.

Note: Figures taken directly from Apple's FY2025 balance sheet above (Total current assets and Total current liabilities).

\[ \text{Current Ratio} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Assets}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Liabilities}} } \]
Meaning

Shows the amount of current assets available relative to current obligations.

Credit interpretation

A higher current ratio generally indicates stronger short-term repayment capacity, but the composition, quality, and convertibility of current assets also matter.

Potential warning signals
  • Declining current ratio
  • The ratio less than 1
  • Liquidity supported mainly by inventory
  • Receivables of questionable collectability
Industry comparison

There is no universally good current ratio. Compare it with historical performance and similar companies.

Quick Ratio

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Cash and cash equivalents = $35,934M

Marketable securities (current) = $18,763M

Accounts receivable, net = $39,777M

Current Liabilities = $165,631M

Quick Ratio = (35,934 + 18,763 + 39,777) ÷ 165,631
= 94,474 ÷ 165,631
= 0.57×

Interpretation: Apple’s quick assets covered approximately 57% of its current liabilities in FY2025. The company would still rely on cash generation or other current assets to cover the remainder. For a lender, the result should be considered alongside trend, industry norms, and the borrower’s cash-flow forecast because it may indicate dependence on ongoing cash generation.

Note: Vendor non-trade receivables and inventory are excluded, consistent with the quick-ratio formula.

\[ \text{Quick Ratio} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Quick Assets}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Liabilities}} } \]

Quick Assets = Cash and Cash Equivalents + Current Marketable Securities + Accounts Receivable, Net

Meaning

Measures immediate liquidity using only the most liquid current assets. Inventory and prepaid expenses are excluded because they are less certain to convert to cash quickly and may not be readily saleable at book value.

Credit interpretation

A stronger quick ratio indicates the borrower can meet near-term obligations without depending on inventory sales.

Potential warning signals
  • Large gap between current ratio and quick ratio (liquidity concentrated in inventory)
  • Declining trend over time
Industry comparison

Acceptable levels vary widely by industry and business model; compare against history and peers.

Cash Ratio

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Cash and cash equivalents = $35,934M

Marketable securities (current) = $18,763M

Current Liabilities = $165,631M

Cash Ratio = (35,934 + 18,763) ÷ 165,631
= 54,697 ÷ 165,631
= 0.33×

Interpretation: Apple had $0.33 of cash and cash equivalents for every $1.00 of current liabilities in FY2025. This means its immediately available cash could cover approximately 33% of its short-term obligations. Although the ratio is below 1.00, this does not necessarily indicate weak liquidity because businesses generally do not hold enough cash to pay all current liabilities immediately. A lender should assess this result alongside Apple’s operating cash flow, marketable securities, access to financing, historical liquidity trends, and upcoming obligations.

Note: This version of the cash ratio includes cash, cash equivalents, and current marketable securities as near-cash assets. It excludes receivables and inventory.

\[ \text{Cash Ratio} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Cash and Cash Equivalents}} + \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Marketable Securities}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Liabilities}} } \]
Meaning

Measures the company's ability to cover current liabilities using only its most liquid assets: cash, cash equivalents, and short-term marketable securities.

Credit interpretation

A higher cash ratio indicates stronger immediate liquidity and less dependence on collecting receivables or selling inventory to meet short-term obligations. A ratio below 1.0× is not automatically a warning sign because many healthy companies continuously generate operating cash flow.

Potential warning signals
  • A declining cash ratio over time
  • Low cash liquidity combined with rising current liabilities
  • Negative operating cash flow
  • Large near-term debt maturities or refinancing needs
Industry comparison

There is no universal target cash ratio. Appropriate levels depend on the company's business model, operating cash-flow stability, access to credit, working-capital cycle, and industry practices.

Working Capital

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Current Assets = $147,957M

Current Liabilities = $165,631M

Working Capital = 147,957 − 165,631
= −$17,674M

Interpretation: Apple's current liabilities exceed current assets, producing negative working capital. This is common for companies with strong cash generation and rapid inventory/receivable turnover, but it is still worth noting explicitly.

\[ \text{Working Capital} = \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Assets}} - \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Liabilities}} \]
Meaning

Unlike the ratios above, working capital is an absolute dollar measure, not a ratio. It shows the dollar cushion of current assets over current liabilities rather than a proportional relationship.

Credit interpretation

Positive working capital generally supports short-term operating flexibility; negative working capital may signal near-term liquidity pressure.

Leverage / Solvency

⌄

Leverage measures how heavily a company relies on debt and other creditor financing relative to its assets or equity. Solvency is the broader question of whether that financial structure is sustainable and whether the company can meet its obligations over the long term. Higher leverage generally increases solvency risk, but leverage should be assessed together with cash flow, debt-service capacity, earnings stability, debt maturities, and industry conditions.

Debt-to-EBITDA

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Commercial paper = $7,979M

Term debt (current) = $12,350M

Term debt (non-current) = $78,328M

EBITDA = $144,748M

Total Interest-Bearing Debt = 7,979 + 12,350 + 78,328 = 98,657
Debt-to-EBITDA = 98,657 ÷ 144,748
= 0.68×

Interpretation: Apple had approximately $0.68 of interest-bearing debt for every $1.00 of EBITDA in FY2025. This indicates that its debt was relatively low compared with its annual operating earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. However, Debt-to-EBITDA should be assessed alongside cash flow, debt-service coverage, debt maturity schedule, historical leverage and industry norms.

Important: A Debt-to-EBITDA ratio should not be interpreted as the exact number of years required to repay debt. EBITDA is not fully available for debt repayment because the business must also fund taxes, working-capital requirements, capital expenditures, distributions and other cash obligations.

\[ \text{Debt-to-EBITDA} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Total Interest-Bearing Debt}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{EBITDA}} } \]

Total interest-bearing debt combines commercial paper and current and non-current term debt from the balance sheet.

EBITDA represents earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization.

Meaning

Debt-to-EBITDA compares the borrower's interest-bearing debt with its operating earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization.

It is a cash-flow leverage measure that helps assess how large the borrower's debt burden is relative to its earnings capacity. A higher ratio generally indicates greater leverage and potentially greater repayment risk.

Potential Warning Signals
  • Debt-to-EBITDA is high or increasing significantly over time.
  • Debt increases without a sustainable increase in EBITDA.
  • EBITDA is declining, volatile or dependent on non-recurring earnings.
  • Adjusted EBITDA relies heavily on aggressive or unsupported add-backs.
  • The borrower is taking on additional debt while debt-service coverage is weakening.
  • Maintenance capital expenditures or working-capital requirements consume a substantial portion of operating cash flow.
  • Debt-to-EBITDA is materially higher than the borrower's historical level or relevant industry peers.
Important Note

The definition of debt and EBITDA may differ across lenders and credit agreements. Analysts should confirm which debt obligations are included and whether any EBITDA adjustments or add-backs are permitted before calculating or comparing the ratio.

Debt-to-Equity

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Commercial paper = $7,979M

Term debt (current) = $12,350M

Term debt (non-current) = $78,328M

Total Shareholders' Equity = $73,733M

Total Interest-Bearing Debt = 7,979 + 12,350 + 78,328 = 98,657
Debt-to-Equity = 98,657 ÷ 73,733
= 1.34×

Interpretation: Apple had $1.34 of total debt for every $1.00 of shareholders’ equity in FY2025. This indicates that debt financing exceeded the company’s reported equity base and that Apple used a relatively leveraged capital structure. From a lender’s perspective, higher leverage increases fixed repayment obligations and can reduce financial flexibility. However, this ratio should be assessed alongside Apple’s operating cash flow, debt-service coverage, profitability, debt maturity schedule, historical leverage, and industry norms.

Note: Figures are taken from Apple’s FY2025 balance sheet above (Total debt and Total shareholders’ equity).

\[ \text{Debt-to-Equity} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Total Interest-Bearing Debt}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Shareholders' Equity}} } \]

Total interest-bearing debt combines commercial paper and current + non-current term debt from the balance sheet above.

Meaning

Compares interest-bearing borrowings with the shareholders' equity supporting the business. A higher ratio indicates greater reliance on borrowed funds relative to owners' capital.

In other words, it shows the extent to which the business is financed through borrowing relative to the equity provided by shareholders and accumulated within the business.

Potential Warning Signals
  • The debt-to-equity ratio is increasing significantly over time.
  • The company is using new borrowing to cover recurring operating cash shortfalls.
  • Interest coverage or debt-service coverage is declining as borrowing increases.
  • A large portion of debt matures in the near term, creating liquidity and refinancing risk.
  • The ratio is materially higher than the company’s historical level or industry peers.
  • Shareholders’ equity is declining, very low, or negative. If equity is negative, the ratio may no longer provide a meaningful measure of leverage.

Debt-to-Assets

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Total Interest-Bearing Debt = $98,657M (7,979 + 12,350 + 78,328)

Total Assets = $359,241M

Debt-to-Assets = 98,657 ÷ 359,241
= 0.27× (≈27.5%)

Interpretation: Approximately 27.5% of Apple’s total assets were supported by interest-bearing debt in FY2025. In other words, Apple had about $0.27 of interest-bearing debt for every $1.00 of assets. From a lender’s perspective, this indicates that most of Apple’s assets were not financed through interest-bearing borrowing. However, the ratio should be assessed alongside Apple’s operating cash flow, debt maturity schedule, interest coverage, asset quality, historical leverage, and industry norms.

Note: Figures are taken from Apple’s FY2025 balance sheet above (Commercial paper, current and non-current term debt, and Total assets).

\[ \text{Debt-to-Assets} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Total Interest-Bearing Debt}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Total Assets}} } \]

Total interest-bearing debt = Commercial paper + Current term debt + Non-current term debt

Meaning

The debt-to-assets ratio shows the proportion of a company’s total assets supported by interest-bearing debt. It measures financial leverage relative to the company’s asset base.

A higher ratio indicates that a larger proportion of the company’s assets is supported by borrowing. This may increase financial risk because interest and principal payments must be made regardless of operating performance.

Potential Warning Signals
  • The ratio increases significantly or persistently over time.
  • Interest-bearing debt grows faster than the company’s assets, revenue, or operating cash flow.
  • The company uses additional borrowing to cover recurring operating cash shortfalls.
  • Interest coverage or debt-service coverage deteriorates as leverage rises.
  • A large amount of debt matures in the near term, creating refinancing and liquidity risk.
  • The assets supporting the company’s debt are declining in value, difficult to sell, or largely intangible.
  • The ratio is materially higher than the company’s historical level or industry peers.

Total Liabilities-to-Equity

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Total Liabilities = $285,508M

Total Shareholders' Equity = $73,733M

Total Liabilities-to-Equity = 285,508 ÷ 73,733
= 3.87×

Interpretation: Apple had $3.87 of total liabilities for every $1.00 of shareholders’ equity in FY2025. This indicates that creditor claims substantially exceeded the company’s reported equity base. From a lender’s perspective, a higher ratio may indicate a smaller equity cushion available to absorb losses. However, the result should be assessed alongside the composition of Apple’s liabilities, operating cash flow, repayment capacity, historical trend, and industry norms.

Note: Figures are taken directly from Apple’s FY2025 balance sheet above (Total liabilities and Total shareholders’ equity).

\[ \text{Total Liabilities-to-Equity} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Total Liabilities}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Shareholders' Equity}} } \]
Meaning

The total liabilities-to-equity ratio compares all obligations owed to creditors with shareholders’ equity. It shows the amount of total liabilities supported by each dollar of reported equity.

This measure is broader than debt-to-equity because it includes both interest-bearing debt and non-interest-bearing operating liabilities, such as accounts payable, deferred revenue, and other liabilities.

Potential Warning Signals
  • The ratio increases significantly or persistently over time.
  • Total liabilities are growing faster than assets, equity, earnings, or operating cash flow.
  • Shareholders’ equity is declining, very low, or negative, reducing the equity cushion available to absorb losses.
  • Current liabilities represent a large share of total liabilities while liquidity and operating cash flow are weakening.
  • Accounts payable or other operating liabilities are increasing because the company is delaying payments to suppliers.
  • The ratio is materially higher than the company’s historical level or the ratios of comparable businesses.

Total liabilities and shareholders’ equity are taken directly from the balance sheet.

Book Leverage

⌄
Illustrative Commercial Borrower

Reported Total Liabilities = $3,200,000

Reported Shareholders’ Equity = $1,400,000

Less: Goodwill and Intangible Assets = $300,000

Less: Unacceptable Related-Party Receivable = $100,000

Add: Postponed Shareholder Loan = $200,000

Adjusted Tangible Net Worth
= 1,400,000 − 300,000 − 100,000 + 200,000
= $1,200,000

Adjusted Liabilities
= 3,200,000 − 200,000
= $3,000,000

Book Leverage = 3,000,000 ÷ 1,200,000
= 2.50×

Interpretation: The borrower has $2.50 of adjusted liabilities for every $1.00 of adjusted tangible net worth. Creditors therefore provide substantially more capital than the owners’ adjusted tangible investment.

Note: This is an illustrative example. A postponed shareholder loan is treated as quasi-equity here and is therefore removed from adjusted liabilities and added to adjusted tangible net worth. Treatment varies by lender.

\[ \text{Book Leverage} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Adjusted Total Liabilities}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Adjusted Tangible Net Worth}} } \]
Meaning

Book leverage measures creditor claims relative to the owners’ adjusted tangible capital. It shows the borrower’s balance-sheet leverage and the tangible equity cushion available to absorb losses.

A higher ratio generally indicates greater dependence on creditors and less tangible owner capital supporting the business.

Typical Adjustments
  • Deduct goodwill and other intangible assets from equity.
  • Deduct related-party or shareholder receivables of uncertain value.
  • Remove asset revaluation amounts not accepted by the lender.
  • Treat properly postponed shareholder debt as quasi-equity when permitted by lender policy.
Potential Warning Signals
  • Book leverage is high or increasing materially.
  • Adjusted tangible net worth is very low or negative.
  • Reported equity consists largely of goodwill or other intangibles.
  • Owners regularly withdraw capital or make substantial distributions.
  • Liabilities grow faster than the borrower’s tangible capital base.
  • The ratio materially exceeds historical results or industry norms.

Cash-Flow Leverage

⌄
Illustrative Commercial Borrower

Operating Line Balance = $700,000

Term Debt = $2,600,000

Finance Lease Obligations = $700,000

Adjusted EBITDA = $1,000,000

Total Funded Debt
= 700,000 + 2,600,000 + 700,000
= $4,000,000

Cash-Flow Leverage = 4,000,000 ÷ 1,000,000
= 4.00×

Interpretation: The borrower has $4.00 of funded debt for every $1.00 of annual adjusted EBITDA. This indicates significant debt relative to its operating earnings and should be compared with lender limits, historical performance and industry norms.

Important: A result of 4.00× does not mean the debt can necessarily be repaid in four years. EBITDA excludes interest, taxes, working-capital needs, capital expenditures and distributions.

\[ \text{Cash-Flow Leverage} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Total Funded Debt}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Adjusted EBITDA}} } \]
Meaning

Cash-flow leverage compares funded debt with the borrower’s adjusted operating earnings. It indicates whether the amount of debt is reasonable relative to the earnings available to support it.

Unlike book leverage, this ratio focuses on earnings capacity rather than the borrower’s balance-sheet equity cushion.

Typical Adjustments
  • Include operating lines, term debt and debt-like lease obligations.
  • Include acquisition debt and other funded obligations where applicable.
  • Remove unsupported, non-recurring or overly optimistic EBITDA add-backs.
  • Normalize unusual owner compensation and legitimate one-time items.
  • Use net debt instead of total funded debt only when permitted by lender policy and the cash is unrestricted and available.
Potential Warning Signals
  • Cash-flow leverage is high or rising significantly.
  • Adjusted EBITDA depends heavily on aggressive add-backs.
  • Debt increases without a sustainable increase in earnings.
  • EBITDA is volatile, declining or concentrated in a few customers.
  • Working-capital requirements or maintenance capital expenditures consume substantial cash.
  • The borrower has limited covenant headroom under a downside scenario.

Assess Book and Cash-Flow Leverage Together

Book leverage evaluates debt or liabilities relative to the borrower’s adjusted tangible capital, while cash-flow leverage evaluates funded debt relative to adjusted EBITDA. Reviewing both provides a more complete view of balance-sheet strength and earnings-based debt capacity.

Formula caution: Banks do not use one universal definition. The credit analysis should state exactly which liabilities, debt obligations, equity adjustments and EBITDA adjustments are included.

Debt vs. Total Liabilities

Interest-bearing debt includes financing obligations such as bank loans, term loans, bonds, notes, and commercial paper.

Total liabilities are broader. They include interest-bearing debt as well as operating and other obligations such as accounts payable, accrued expenses, deferred revenue, taxes payable, and other liabilities.

Higher leverage increases financial obligations and generally reduces the borrower's ability to absorb declining earnings or cash flow. However, there is no universally acceptable leverage ratio. The analyst should consider the company's industry, historical trend, cash generation, repayment capacity, debt maturity profile, and overall financial risk.

Debt Service / Coverage Particularly important for commercial credit analysis

⌄

Debt Service / Coverage mmeasures a company’s ability to generate enough earnings or cash flow to meet its debt obligations, including interest and principal payments.

Interest Coverage Ratio

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

EBIT (Operating Income) = $133,050M

Interest Expense = not separately disclosed

Cannot be calculated precisely

Apple's Consolidated Statement of Operations on this page does not break out interest expense as its own line item — it is folded into "Other income/(expense), net" (−$321M), which also includes interest income and other non-operating items. Without a disclosed interest expense figure, this ratio cannot be calculated accurately from the data shown here, and no value should be invented.

\[ \text{Interest Coverage Ratio} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{EBIT}} }{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{Interest Expense}} } \]

EBIT: Earnings Before Interest and Taxes (Operating Profit)

Meaning

How many times can operating earnings cover the borrower's interest expense?

Watch for

Declining coverage may indicate less financial flexibility, especially if interest expense is rising faster than EBIT.

Where to find Interest Expense

Interest expense may be reported directly on the Income Statement. If it is not shown as a separate line item, check the Notes to the Financial Statements, where the company may provide a breakdown of finance costs, interest expense, or other income/expense.

EBITDA Interest Coverage

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

EBIT (Operating Income) = $133,050M

Depreciation and amortization = $11,698M

EBITDA = 133,050 + 11,698 = 144,748

Interest Expense = not separately disclosed

EBITDA = $144,748M; ratio cannot be completed

EBITDA can be built from the income statement and cash flow statement shown on this page, but interest expense is not broken out separately (see the Interest Coverage Ratio note above), so the full EBITDA-to-interest ratio cannot be calculated accurately here.

\[ \text{EBITDA Interest Coverage Ratio} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{EBIT}} \color{#a73193}{\text{DA}} }{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{Interest Expense}} } \]

EBITDA = Operating Income (EBIT) + Depreciation + Amortization.

Note

EBITDA is useful as a pre-interest, pre-tax operating performance measure, but it is not the same as cash flow because it does not account for capital expenditures, working-capital requirements, taxes, or principal repayments.

Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

EBITDA = 133,050 + 11,698 = 144,748

Unfinanced Capital Expenditures = not separately identified

Distributions = not separately identified for this DSCR definition

Required Principal Repayments = not separately identified

Interest Expense = not separately disclosed

Cannot be calculated reliably

Apple FY2025 EBITDA can be calculated from the financial statements shown earlier on this page and equals $144,748M. However, the lender-style DSCR used here also requires unfinanced capital expenditures, distributions, required principal repayments, and interest expense. These inputs are not all separately identifiable from the figures presented here, so the full DSCR cannot be calculated reliably.

\[ \text{DSCR} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{ \text{Cash flow available for debt service​} } }{ \color{#0c7973}{ \text{Total Debt Service } } } \]

Cash flow available for debt service = EBITDA - Unfinanced CapEx -Distributions

or

Cash flow available for debt service = Net Income + Deferred Taxes + Depreciation + Interest on Debt

EBITDA = Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortization
Unfinanced CapEx = Capital expenditures funded from the borrower’s own cash rather than from new financing
Distributions = Cash withdrawn from the business by owners or shareholders, including dividends and other owner distributions
Total Debt Service = Required Principal Repayments + Interest Expense
What it shows

DSCR measures whether the cash earnings available after necessary unfinanced capital expenditures and owner distributions are sufficient to cover the borrower’s required principal and interest payments.

Interpretation

A higher DSCR generally indicates stronger repayment capacity and a larger financial buffer against weaker earnings, higher expenses, or other adverse changes. A lower DSCR indicates tighter repayment capacity and greater credit risk.

Reading the result
  • DSCR > 1: adjusted cash available for debt service exceeds required debt service.
  • DSCR = 1: adjusted cash available for debt service exactly covers required debt service.
  • DSCR < 1: adjusted cash available for debt service is insufficient to cover required debt service.
Potential warning signals
  • A DSCR below 1.00.
  • A consistently declining DSCR.
  • EBITDA growing more slowly than required debt service.
  • High unfinanced capital expenditures reducing cash available for repayment.
  • Significant owner distributions weakening debt-service capacity.
  • Increasing interest expense or required principal repayments.
  • A DSCR only slightly above the lender’s minimum requirement.
  • Dependence on unusually high or non-recurring EBITDA.
Important note

DSCR definitions vary across lenders, industries, and credit agreements. Some lenders may use different adjustments to EBITDA, capital expenditures, distributions, taxes, or debt-service obligations. The applicable lender definition should therefore always be confirmed before calculating or comparing DSCR.

No single DSCR threshold is universally acceptable. Requirements such as 1.20x or 1.25x are common examples, but the actual minimum is typically set by the lender, industry, borrower risk profile, and credit agreement.

Maintenance CapEx vs. Total CapEx

Not all capital expenditures have the same effect on debt-service analysis. Analysts often distinguish between maintenance CapEx and growth CapEx.

  • Maintenance CapEx: spending required to maintain the company’s existing productive capacity, replace worn-out equipment, and continue normal operations.
  • Growth CapEx: discretionary or expansionary spending intended to increase capacity, enter new markets, improve productivity, or support future growth.
  • Total CapEx: includes both maintenance and growth capital expenditures.

In credit analysis, maintenance CapEx is especially important because it represents a recurring cash requirement that the borrower may not be able to defer indefinitely. If it is ignored, cash available for debt service may be overstated.

ROCE

\[ \text{ROCE} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{EBIT}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Capital Employed}} } \times 100\% \]
Where

EBIT = Earnings Before Interest and Taxes, and commonly Capital Employed = Total Assets − Current Liabilities.

Conceptually

Why it matters
  • Assesses operating efficiency of long-term capital
  • Declining ROCE may indicate deteriorating performance
  • Compare with historical ROCE and industry peers
Potential Warning Signals
  • Declining or negative ROCE.
  • ROCE below historical or industry levels.
  • Capital employed growing faster than operating profit.
  • An artificially high ROCE caused by a very small capital base.

ROCE

Considers returns generated using both equity and long-term financing (the full capital base employed in the business).

ROE

Focuses on returns generated relative specifically to shareholders' equity.

Inventory Turnover

⌄

The inventory turnover ratio shows how many times a business sells and replaces its stock over a period.

\[ \text{Inventory Turnover} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{COGS}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Average Inventory}} } \]
Where

COGS = Cost of Goods Sold during the period, and Average Inventory is commonly calculated as:
(Beginning Inventory + Ending Inventory) ÷ 2 .

Conceptually

Inventory Turnover measures how many times a company sells and replaces its inventory during a period. It helps assess how efficiently inventory is being managed and converted into sales.

Why it matters
  • Helps assess how efficiently the company manages its inventory
  • Low or declining turnover may indicate slow-moving or obsolete inventory, overstocking, or weaker demand
  • Very high turnover may indicate strong inventory efficiency, but could also signal insufficient inventory levels
  • Compare with historical turnover and industry peers because normal inventory turnover varies significantly by industry

Total Cost of Sales (COGS) = $ 220,960M

Beginning Inventory (FY2024 ending balance) = $ 7,286M

Ending Inventory (FY2025 ending balance) = $ 5,718M

Average Inventory = (7,286 + 5,718) ÷ 2 = 6,502
Inventory Turnover = 220,960 ÷ 6,502
≈ 33.98×

Interpretation: That means Apple sells and replaces its average inventory approximately 33.98 times during the year.

Note: Beginning and ending inventory are taken from Apple's comparative Balance Sheet. For FY2025, the FY2024 ending inventory balance of $7,286M serves as beginning inventory, while the FY2025 balance of $5,718M is ending inventory. Perform the Commercial Credit Assessment

⌄

Now that we are familiar with financial statements and financial ratios, it is time to walk through the practical process of assessing the creditworthiness of a commercial financing applicant.

?

Central Credit Question

How will the borrower repay the loan, and what could prevent that repayment?

Financial ratios are essential, but a credit decision should never be based on ratios alone. A commercial credit analyst must also consider the borrower’s business, the purpose of the financing, historical financial performance, cash flow, existing debt obligations, management quality, industry and economic conditions, collateral, guarantees, the proposed loan structure, and the key risks affecting repayment.

The 5 Cs of Credit provide a widely used theoretical framework for bringing this financial and qualitative evidence together:

The Five Components

  • Character

    The borrower’s integrity, reliability, experience, and history of meeting financial obligations.

  • Capacity

    The business’s ability to generate sufficient cash flow to repay the proposed loan.

  • Capital

    The owners’ financial investment in the business and the borrower’s overall financial strength.

  • Collateral

    The assets and guarantees available to provide secondary repayment support if the borrower defaults.

  • Conditions

    The purpose and terms of the financing, together with industry, market, and economic conditions that could affect the borrower.

The 5 Cs help analysts organize and evaluate the evidence gathered during a credit assessment. However, they do not specify the order in which the analysis should be performed. For this purpose, we use a practical 12-step commercial credit assessment process that guides the analysis from the initial financing request to the final credit recommendation.

Below, we explain the purpose and key considerations of each step. In Section 5, you will apply the complete process to hypothetical commercial financing applications and prepare credit recommendations.

Commercial Credit Assessment Process

⌄

A commercial credit assessment can be organized into the following 12-step analytical process. The exact process and terminology may differ across lenders.

1. Borrower, Business & Management Ownership · Business model · Character
2. Purpose & Financing Request Use of funds · Amount · Facility type
3. Historical Revenue & Profitability Revenue · Margins · Earnings quality
4. Historical Liquidity & Working Capital Liquidity · Receivables · Inventory · Payables
5. Historical Leverage & Capital Debt burden · Equity · Financial cushion
6. Historical Cash Flow & Debt Service Cash generation · DSCR · Coverage
7. Pro Forma Performance Forecasts · Future ratios · Repayment capacity
8. Sensitivity & Downside Analysis Stress scenarios · Break-even · Resilience
9. Industry, Market & Economic Risks Competition · Industry · External conditions
10. Collateral & Guarantees Security · Recovery · Secondary repayment
11. Facility Structure & Covenants Term · Amortization · Conditions · Monitoring
12. Recommendation & Monitoring Plan Approve · Modify · Decline · Monitor

Historical financial analysis is divided across Steps 3–6. These areas are presented separately for clarity, but they must ultimately be evaluated together.

1. Understand the Borrower, Business and Management

⌄

Establish who owns and controls the borrower, how the business generates cash, what could interrupt its operations, and whether management is capable, reliable and transparent.

?

Central Question

How does the business generate repayment cash, and can the lender rely on the people managing it?

Questions to Ask

  • Who legally owns and controls the borrower?
  • What products or services generate revenue?
  • Who are the principal customers and suppliers?
  • Is the business seasonal, cyclical or concentrated?
  • What experience does management have?
  • Has the borrower paid previous obligations as agreed?
  • Are taxes, licences and regulatory filings current?

Information and Sources

  • Corporate registration and ownership records
  • Business plan and organizational chart
  • Customer and supplier contracts
  • Management résumés
  • Business and personal credit reports
  • Bank and existing loan records
  • Tax, court, judgment and lien searches

Potential Warning Signals Signals

  • Undisclosed owners, debts or legal proceedings
  • Information that conflicts with supporting records
  • Repeated late payments or unpaid taxes
  • Excessive dependence on one manager
  • Weak records or unrealistic explanations
  • Resistance to reasonable information requests

Output of Step 1

  • Verified borrower and ownership profile
  • Summary of the business model and key dependencies
  • Assessment of management capability and character
  • Identification of important operational risks

2. Understand the Purpose and Financing Request

⌄

Determine exactly why the borrower needs financing, how the amount was calculated, how the funds will be used and whether the proposed facility matches the financing need.

?

Central Question

Is the financing purpose legitimate, economically sensible and appropriately structured?

Eligible leased asset cost or value and expected lease payments, subject to advance limits and lessee credit quality. Assess lessee credit quality, lessee concentration, lease terms, equipment useful life, residual-value assumptions, remarketing risk and the assignment of lease payments.
Project Finance Structured Cash-Flow Based Finances a major project primarily from the future cash flow generated by that project. Projected project cash flow under base and downside cases, constrained by debt-service coverage and project funding needs. Review construction and completion risk, projected cash flow, contractual arrangements, cost overruns, operator capability, counterparty risk and sensitivity to price, volume and delays.
Franchise Financing Specialized Asset Financing Finances the purchase, opening, renovation or expansion of a franchise location. Eligible startup or purchase costs, required owner equity, collateral value and sustainable store-level repayment capacity. Review the franchise agreement, franchisor strength, location, royalties, required renovations, store-level performance, operator experience and restrictions on transfer or termination.
Acquisition Financing Transaction Cash-Flow Based Finances the purchase of another company, business division or group of operating assets. Purchase price and funding gap after buyer equity, constrained by sustainable combined cash flow and post-acquisition leverage. Analyze purchase-price reasonableness, goodwill, normalized EBITDA, post-acquisition leverage, integration risk, expected synergies, management capacity and downside projections.
Management Buyout Financing Transaction Cash-Flow Based Helps the existing management team acquire the business from its current owners. Purchase price less management equity and seller financing, constrained by post-closing cash flow and leverage. Assess management’s equity contribution, leadership capability, reliance on the departing owner, seller financing, post-closing leverage and whether debt service leaves sufficient operating liquidity.
Leveraged or Sponsor-Backed Lending Transaction Structured Finances highly leveraged businesses, often in connection with a private-equity acquisition. Transaction funding need after sponsor equity, constrained by sustainable EBITDA, leverage and debt-service capacity. Examine total leverage, EBITDA adjustments, sponsor equity, subordinated debt, covenant headroom, distributions, refinancing risk and repayment capacity under downside scenarios.
Syndicated Lending Structured Large Exposure Allows several financial institutions to share a large credit facility. Underlying facility size determined by its loan type and borrower capacity; each lender takes an agreed share. Review the agent bank’s responsibilities, voting rights, shared security, inter-lender arrangements, information rights, hold level and the lender’s ability to influence amendments or enforcement.
Government-Supported Lending Government Supported Specialized Provides financing supported by a government guarantee, insurance program or risk-sharing arrangement. Eligible financing need and program limits, subject to borrower contribution and repayment capacity; the guarantee supports lender risk. Confirm borrower and loan eligibility, permitted uses of funds, documentation, registration, reporting and claim requirements. A government guarantee does not eliminate the need to assess repayment capacity.

Historical Financial Analysis: Steps 3–6

Historical financial analysis includes revenue and profitability, liquidity and working capital, leverage and capital structure, and cash flow and debt-service capacity. These areas are separated to make the process easier to follow, but the findings must be evaluated together.

⌄

Examine whether revenue, margins and earnings are improving, stable, volatile or deteriorating, and determine whether reported profits represent sustainable operating performance.

?

Central Question

Questions and Analysis

  • Is revenue growing, stable, declining or volatile?

  • Are profits generated by normal normal operations?

10. Evaluate Collateral and Guarantees

⌄

Determine what secondary repayment support is available and what the lender could realistically recover after considering prior claims, realization costs and delays.

?

Central Question

If the primary repayment source fails, what can the lender realistically recover?

A lender may have two forms of secondary repayment support: collateral security and guarantees. Collateral security gives the lender a claim against specified assets. A guarantee is a separate promise by a person or another company to pay if the borrower does not meet its obligations.

  • General Security Agreement (GSA): A document that gives the lender a security interest in the borrower’s business personal property, as described in the agreement. This may include receivables, inventory and equipment.
  • Personal guarantee: An individual, often an owner, promises to answer for the company’s debt under the terms of the guarantee.
  • Corporate guarantee: A separate company, such as a parent or affiliate, promises to answer for the borrower’s debt under the terms of the guarantee.
  • Specific asset security: A lender may take security over an identified asset, such as a vehicle or piece of equipment. The security interest may be registered in Ontario’s Personal Property Security Registration system so that other lenders can discover the claim. For a motor vehicle, its vehicle identification number (VIN) helps identify the specific vehicle; other equipment may be identified by a manufacturer’s serial number. The lender checks for existing liens and confirms its priority.

The analyst should verify what each document covers, whether the security has been properly registered, the lender’s priority, and whether each guarantor has the financial capacity to provide support.

Questions and Analysis

  • Who legally owns the collateral?
  • What is its current market value (CMV) The estimated price the collateral could sell for under normal market conditions, with adequate time to market the asset. and orderly liquidation value (OLV) The estimated amount the collateral could sell for when the lender has limited, but reasonable, time to market and sell the asset after default. OLV is normally lower than CMV. ?
  • How quickly could it be sold?
  • Are there prior-ranking liens A prior-ranking lien gives another creditor a higher-priority claim on the collateral. If the asset is sold after a default, that creditor generally has a claim to the proceeds before a lower-ranking lender. on the proposed collateral?
  • If so, has the existing creditor agreed to subordinate its claim Under a subordination agreement, a creditor agrees that another creditor’s claim will have priority, subject to the agreement’s terms and the assets it covers. ?
  • Are any owner or related-party loans subject to a subordination and postponement agreement A subordination and postponement agreement requires the owner or related-party creditor to rank behind the bank and not receive repayment until the bank has been repaid or gives written consent. This preserves cash for the business and protects the bank’s repayment priority. ?
  • Is the collateral specialized

    Specialized: The asset has few potential buyers. A custom machine built for one factory may be valuable to that borrower but difficult to sell to anyone else.

    or volatile

    Volatile: The asset’s market price can change quickly. Commodity inventory, for example, may be worth much less by the time the lender sells it.

    ?
  • Is it adequately insured?
  • Do guarantors have sufficient net worth and liquidity?

  • Independent appraisals showing CMV and OLV
  • Purchase agreements and asset listings
  • Title, lien and security searches
  • Receivable and inventory reports
  • Insurance certificates
  • Environmental assessments
  • Guarantor financial statements and credit reports

11. Structure the Facility and Establish Covenants

⌄

Design facility terms that match the financing purpose, asset life, cash-flow cycle and identified risks.

?

Central Question

What structure supports the borrower’s financing need while appropriately controlling the lender’s risk?

Facility Terms

  • Facility type and amount
  • Term and amortization period Term

    The period until the loan matures and the remaining balance becomes due or must be refinanced.

    Amortization

    The period over which payments are calculated to fully repay the loan.

    Balloon Payment

    The remaining loan balance due at the end of the term when the amortization period is longer than the term.

    Example

    A $500,000 loan has a 5-year term and a 10-year amortization. Payments are calculated over 10 years, but after 5 years the loan matures. The remaining balance is the balloon payment and must be repaid or refinanced.

  • Interest rate and fees
  • Payment frequency
  • Grace period where justified Grace Period

    Grace Period: A specified period at the beginning of a loan during which the borrower is temporarily not required to make principal payments, or in some cases any payments. Interest may continue to accrue or remain payable during this period.

    Example

    A company receives an equipment loan with a 6-month principal grace period. During the first 6 months, it pays interest only. Regular principal repayments begin after the grace period.

  • Availability period and staged advances

    Availability Period: The specified period during which the borrower may draw approved funds under the facility.

    Staged Advances : Loan funds are released in portions rather than all at once, usually as specified conditions or project milestones are satisfied.

    Example: A $2M construction loan has a 12-month availability period. Instead of receiving $2M immediately, the borrower receives advances as construction milestones are completed and verified.

  • Borrowing-base

    A borrowing base is the maximum amount the borrower may draw under a revolving operating line. It is calculated using eligible accounts receivable and inventory, after applying the bank’s advance rates and eligibility criteria.

    Example

    Eligible accounts receivable of $800,000 at an 80% advance rate provides $640,000 of availability. Eligible inventory of $400,000 at a 50% advance rate provides another $200,000. The total borrowing base is $840,000.

    or clean-up requirements

    A clean-up requirement requires an operating line to be fully or substantially repaid for a specified period each year. It helps confirm that the line finances short-term working-capital needs, rather than permanent losses or long-term assets.

  • Cash sweep

    A cash sweep requires the borrower to apply an agreed portion of excess cash flow, asset-sale proceeds, insurance proceeds, or other specified cash receipts to repay debt before scheduled maturity.

  • Conditions precedent (CPs)

    Conditions precedent are requirements the borrower must satisfy before the bank is obligated to fund a loan or permit the first advance. They ensure that the approved structure, security, and risk controls are in place before money is released.

    Typical CPs include signed loan and security documents, satisfactory lien searches, proof of insurance, required appraisals or environmental assessments, and the borrower’s required equity contribution.

Conditions and Covenants

  • Owner equity invested before funding
  • Required security and insurance
  • Minimum DSCR
  • Maximum leverage
  • Minimum tangible net worth
  • Restrictions on distributions and new debt
  • Maximum loan-to-value (LTV), where applicable Applicable to asset-backed financing, such as Commercial Real Estate / Mortgage Lending and Equipment Financing, where the loan amount is assessed relative to the value of the underlying asset.
  • Financial reporting requirements

Practical Questions

  • Does amortization reflect expected cash generation?
  • Are covenants measurable and clearly defined?
  • Do thresholds provide sufficient early warning?
  • Can compliance be monitored promptly?
  • Can the borrower comply at the time of approval?
  • Are exceptions and approval authorities documented?

Output of Step 11

  • Recommended facility structure and pricing
  • Security and conditions precedent
  • Financial and restrictive covenants
  • Reporting requirements and monitoring triggers

Approve

Repayment capacity and overall credit risk are acceptable under the proposed facility structure.

Repayment capacity or overall credit risk remains unacceptable even after reasonable structural changes or mitigants are considered.
