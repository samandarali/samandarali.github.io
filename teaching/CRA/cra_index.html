Introduction to Commercial Credit Analysis

⌄

When a business requests credit, the lender must determine whether the loan can be repaid as agreed—not simply whether the business is profitable or owns valuable assets. Commercial credit analysis provides a structured process for making that determination.

Central Credit Question

Can the borrower repay the loan as agreed, and what could prevent repayment?

What this page covers

This page introduces the main stages of commercial credit analysis. We begin by examining the four principal financial statements and using a real-company example to understand what each statement reveals about financial performance, financial position, and cash flow. We then calculate and interpret key ratios used to assess profitability, liquidity, leverage, debt-service coverage, and operating efficiency.

Building on this financial foundation, we bring the findings together in a complete credit assessment. This includes understanding the borrower and the purpose of the financing, evaluating repayment capacity, identifying financial and qualitative risks, reviewing projections and collateral, assessing the proposed loan structure, and forming an evidence-based credit recommendation. Finally, a practical credit case provides an opportunity to apply the complete process.

Understand the Financial Statements

⌄

Financial statements are formal accounting reports that provide information about a company's financial position, financial performance, cash flows, and changes in equity.

Four Main Financial Statements

⌄

The four main financial statements are the Balance Sheet, Income Statement, Cash Flow Statement, and Statement of Changes in Equity. Below, we examine what each statement shows and why it matters in commercial credit analysis.

Balance Sheet

Shows the company's financial position at a specific point in time - what it owns, what it owes, and the owners' residual interest.

Key information
Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity
Credit question:
How liquid, leveraged, and financially strong is the borrower?

Cash Flow Statement

Shows the company's actual cash inflows and outflows during a period.

Key information
Operating activities
Investing activities
Financing activities
Credit question:
Does the business actually generate enough cash to service its debt?

Important: A profitable company can still experience repayment problems if its operations do not generate sufficient cash.

Income Statement

Shows the company's financial performance over a period and whether its operations generate profit.

Key information
Revenue → COGS → Gross Profit → Operating Expenses → EBIT → Interest → Taxes → Net Income
Credit question:
Is the borrower generating sufficient and sustainable earnings?

Statement of Changes in Equity

Explains how shareholders’ equity changed during the period.

Key information
Net income · Dividends · Share issuance · Share repurchases · Other comprehensive income
Credit question:
What is causing the owners’ financial interest in the business to increase or decrease?

Why lenders analyze the statements together

For credit analysis, financial statements should not be analyzed independently. The income statement shows whether the borrower is profitable, the balance sheet shows its financial position and leverage, and the cash flow statement shows whether the borrower actually generates enough cash to service its obligations. The statement of changes in equity helps explain how profits, dividends, share transactions, and other changes affect the owners' financial interest in the business.

Income StatementProfitability

Analyze and Interpret Financial Ratios

⌄

Financial Ratio Analysis

⌄

Ratios should rarely be interpreted in isolation. A commercial credit analyst normally compares them with the borrower's historical performance, industry averages, competitors, lending covenants, and the characteristics of the business.

Liquidity

⌄

Liquidity measures the borrower's ability to meet short-term obligations when they become due.

Current Ratio

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Current Assets = $147,957M

Current Liabilities = $165,631M

Current Ratio = 147,957 ÷ 165,631
= 0.89×

Interpretation: Apple had $0.89 of current assets for every $1.00 of current liabilities in FY2025. This indicates negative working capital and suggests that Apple depends partly on continuing operating cash inflows and access to financing to meet its short-term obligations. A lender should assess this result alongside Apple’s historical trend, industry norms, operating cash flow, and liquidity forecast.

Note: Figures taken directly from Apple's FY2025 balance sheet above (Total current assets and Total current liabilities).

\[ \text{Current Ratio} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Assets}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Liabilities}} } \]
Meaning

Shows the amount of current assets available relative to current obligations.

Credit interpretation

A higher current ratio generally indicates stronger short-term repayment capacity, but the composition, quality, and convertibility of current assets also matter.

Potential warning signals
  • Declining current ratio
  • The ratio less than 1
  • Liquidity supported mainly by inventory
  • Receivables of questionable collectability
Industry comparison

There is no universally good current ratio. Compare it with historical performance and similar companies.

Quick Ratio

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Cash and cash equivalents = $35,934M

Marketable securities (current) = $18,763M

Accounts receivable, net = $39,777M

Current Liabilities = $165,631M

Quick Ratio = (35,934 + 18,763 + 39,777) ÷ 165,631
= 94,474 ÷ 165,631
= 0.57×

Interpretation: Apple’s quick assets covered approximately 57% of its current liabilities in FY2025. The company would still rely on cash generation or other current assets to cover the remainder. For a lender, the result should be considered alongside trend, industry norms, and the borrower’s cash-flow forecast because it may indicate dependence on ongoing cash generation.

Note: Vendor non-trade receivables and inventory are excluded, consistent with the quick-ratio formula.

\[ \text{Quick Ratio} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Quick Assets}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Liabilities}} } \]

Quick Assets = Cash and Cash Equivalents + Current Marketable Securities + Accounts Receivable, Net

Meaning

Measures immediate liquidity using only the most liquid current assets. Inventory and prepaid expenses are excluded because they are less certain to convert to cash quickly and may not be readily saleable at book value.

Credit interpretation

A stronger quick ratio indicates the borrower can meet near-term obligations without depending on inventory sales.

Potential warning signals
  • Large gap between current ratio and quick ratio (liquidity concentrated in inventory)
  • Declining trend over time
Industry comparison

Acceptable levels vary widely by industry and business model; compare against history and peers.

Cash Ratio

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Cash and cash equivalents = $35,934M

Marketable securities (current) = $18,763M

Current Liabilities = $165,631M

Cash Ratio = (35,934 + 18,763) ÷ 165,631
= 54,697 ÷ 165,631
= 0.33×

Interpretation: Apple had $0.33 of cash and cash equivalents for every $1.00 of current liabilities in FY2025. This means its immediately available cash could cover approximately 33% of its short-term obligations. Although the ratio is below 1.00, this does not necessarily indicate weak liquidity because businesses generally do not hold enough cash to pay all current liabilities immediately. A lender should assess this result alongside Apple’s operating cash flow, marketable securities, access to financing, historical liquidity trends, and upcoming obligations.

Note: This version of the cash ratio includes cash, cash equivalents, and current marketable securities as near-cash assets. It excludes receivables and inventory.

\[ \text{Cash Ratio} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Cash and Cash Equivalents}} + \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Marketable Securities}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Liabilities}} } \]
Meaning

Measures the company's ability to cover current liabilities using only its most liquid assets: cash, cash equivalents, and short-term marketable securities.

Credit interpretation

A higher cash ratio indicates stronger immediate liquidity and less dependence on collecting receivables or selling inventory to meet short-term obligations. A ratio below 1.0× is not automatically a warning sign because many healthy companies continuously generate operating cash flow.

Potential warning signals
  • A declining cash ratio over time
  • Low cash liquidity combined with rising current liabilities
  • Negative operating cash flow
  • Large near-term debt maturities or refinancing needs
Industry comparison

There is no universal target cash ratio. Appropriate levels depend on the company's business model, operating cash-flow stability, access to credit, working-capital cycle, and industry practices.

Working Capital

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Current Assets = $147,957M

Current Liabilities = $165,631M

Working Capital = 147,957 − 165,631
= −$17,674M

Interpretation: Apple's current liabilities exceed current assets, producing negative working capital. This is common for companies with strong cash generation and rapid inventory/receivable turnover, but it is still worth noting explicitly.

\[ \text{Working Capital} = \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Assets}} - \color{#1b59df}{\text{Current Liabilities}} \]
Meaning

Unlike the ratios above, working capital is an absolute dollar measure, not a ratio. It shows the dollar cushion of current assets over current liabilities rather than a proportional relationship.

Credit interpretation

Positive working capital generally supports short-term operating flexibility; negative working capital may signal near-term liquidity pressure.

Leverage / Solvency

⌄

Leverage measures how heavily a company relies on debt and other creditor financing relative to its assets or equity. Solvency is the broader question of whether that financial structure is sustainable and whether the company can meet its obligations over the long term. Higher leverage generally increases solvency risk, but leverage should be assessed together with cash flow, debt-service capacity, earnings stability, debt maturities, and industry conditions.

Debt-to-Equity

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Commercial paper = $7,979M

Term debt (current) = $12,350M

Term debt (non-current) = $78,328M

Total Shareholders' Equity = $73,733M

Total Interest-Bearing Debt = 7,979 + 12,350 + 78,328 = 98,657
Debt-to-Equity = 98,657 ÷ 73,733
= 1.34×

Interpretation: Apple had $1.34 of total debt for every $1.00 of shareholders’ equity in FY2025. This indicates that debt financing exceeded the company’s reported equity base and that Apple used a relatively leveraged capital structure. From a lender’s perspective, higher leverage increases fixed repayment obligations and can reduce financial flexibility. However, this ratio should be assessed alongside Apple’s operating cash flow, debt-service coverage, profitability, debt maturity schedule, historical leverage, and industry norms.

Note: Figures are taken from Apple’s FY2025 balance sheet above (Total debt and Total shareholders’ equity).

\[ \text{Debt-to-Equity} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Total Interest-Bearing Debt}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Shareholders' Equity}} } \]

Total interest-bearing debt combines commercial paper and current + non-current term debt from the balance sheet above.

Meaning

Compares interest-bearing borrowings with the shareholders' equity supporting the business. A higher ratio indicates greater reliance on borrowed funds relative to owners' capital.

In other words, it shows the extent to which the business is financed through borrowing relative to the equity provided by shareholders and accumulated within the business.

Potential Warning Signals
  • The debt-to-equity ratio is increasing significantly over time.
  • The company is using new borrowing to cover recurring operating cash shortfalls.
  • Interest coverage or debt-service coverage is declining as borrowing increases.
  • A large portion of debt matures in the near term, creating liquidity and refinancing risk.
  • The ratio is materially higher than the company’s historical level or industry peers.
  • Shareholders’ equity is declining, very low, or negative. If equity is negative, the ratio may no longer provide a meaningful measure of leverage.

Debt-to-Assets

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Total Interest-Bearing Debt = $98,657M (7,979 + 12,350 + 78,328)

Total Assets = $359,241M

Debt-to-Assets = 98,657 ÷ 359,241
= 0.27× (≈27.5%)

Interpretation: Approximately 27.5% of Apple’s total assets were supported by interest-bearing debt in FY2025. In other words, Apple had about $0.27 of interest-bearing debt for every $1.00 of assets. From a lender’s perspective, this indicates that most of Apple’s assets were not financed through interest-bearing borrowing. However, the ratio should be assessed alongside Apple’s operating cash flow, debt maturity schedule, interest coverage, asset quality, historical leverage, and industry norms.

Note: Figures are taken from Apple’s FY2025 balance sheet above (Commercial paper, current and non-current term debt, and Total assets).

\[ \text{Debt-to-Assets} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Total Interest-Bearing Debt}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Total Assets}} } \]

Total interest-bearing debt = Commercial paper + Current term debt + Non-current term debt

Meaning

The debt-to-assets ratio shows the proportion of a company’s total assets supported by interest-bearing debt. It measures financial leverage relative to the company’s asset base.

A higher ratio indicates that a larger proportion of the company’s assets is supported by borrowing. This may increase financial risk because interest and principal payments must be made regardless of operating performance.

Potential Warning Signals
  • The ratio increases significantly or persistently over time.
  • Interest-bearing debt grows faster than the company’s assets, revenue, or operating cash flow.
  • The company uses additional borrowing to cover recurring operating cash shortfalls.
  • Interest coverage or debt-service coverage deteriorates as leverage rises.
  • A large amount of debt matures in the near term, creating refinancing and liquidity risk.
  • The assets supporting the company’s debt are declining in value, difficult to sell, or largely intangible.
  • The ratio is materially higher than the company’s historical level or industry peers.

Total Liabilities-to-Equity

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Total Liabilities = $285,508M

Total Shareholders' Equity = $73,733M

Total Liabilities-to-Equity = 285,508 ÷ 73,733
= 3.87×

Interpretation: Apple had $3.87 of total liabilities for every $1.00 of shareholders’ equity in FY2025. This indicates that creditor claims substantially exceeded the company’s reported equity base. From a lender’s perspective, a higher ratio may indicate a smaller equity cushion available to absorb losses. However, the result should be assessed alongside the composition of Apple’s liabilities, operating cash flow, repayment capacity, historical trend, and industry norms.

Note: Figures are taken directly from Apple’s FY2025 balance sheet above (Total liabilities and Total shareholders’ equity).

\[ \text{Total Liabilities-to-Equity} = \frac{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Total Liabilities}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Shareholders' Equity}} } \]
Meaning

The total liabilities-to-equity ratio compares all obligations owed to creditors with shareholders’ equity. It shows the amount of total liabilities supported by each dollar of reported equity.

This measure is broader than debt-to-equity because it includes both interest-bearing debt and non-interest-bearing operating liabilities, such as accounts payable, deferred revenue, and other liabilities.

Potential Warning Signals
  • The ratio increases significantly or persistently over time.
  • Total liabilities are growing faster than assets, equity, earnings, or operating cash flow.
  • Shareholders’ equity is declining, very low, or negative, reducing the equity cushion available to absorb losses.
  • Current liabilities represent a large share of total liabilities while liquidity and operating cash flow are weakening.
  • Accounts payable or other operating liabilities are increasing because the company is delaying payments to suppliers.
  • The ratio is materially higher than the company’s historical level or the ratios of comparable businesses.

Total liabilities and shareholders’ equity are taken directly from the balance sheet.

Debt vs. Total Liabilities

Interest-bearing debt includes financing obligations such as bank loans, term loans, bonds, notes, and commercial paper.

Total liabilities are broader. They include interest-bearing debt as well as operating and other obligations such as accounts payable, accrued expenses, deferred revenue, taxes payable, and other liabilities.

Higher leverage increases financial obligations and generally reduces the borrower's ability to absorb declining earnings or cash flow. However, there is no universally acceptable leverage ratio. The analyst should consider the company's industry, historical trend, cash generation, repayment capacity, debt maturity profile, and overall financial risk.

Debt Service / Coverage Particularly important for commercial credit analysis

⌄

Debt Service / Coverage mmeasures a company’s ability to generate enough earnings or cash flow to meet its debt obligations, including interest and principal payments.

Interest Coverage Ratio

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

EBIT (Operating Income) = $133,050M

Interest Expense = not separately disclosed

Cannot be calculated precisely

Apple's Consolidated Statement of Operations on this page does not break out interest expense as its own line item — it is folded into "Other income/(expense), net" (−$321M), which also includes interest income and other non-operating items. Without a disclosed interest expense figure, this ratio cannot be calculated accurately from the data shown here, and no value should be invented.

\[ \text{Interest Coverage Ratio} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{EBIT}} }{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{Interest Expense}} } \]

EBIT: Earnings Before Interest and Taxes (Operating Profit)

Meaning

How many times can operating earnings cover the borrower's interest expense?

Watch for

Declining coverage may indicate less financial flexibility, especially if interest expense is rising faster than EBIT.

Where to find Interest Expense

Interest expense may be reported directly on the Income Statement. If it is not shown as a separate line item, check the Notes to the Financial Statements, where the company may provide a breakdown of finance costs, interest expense, or other income/expense.

EBITDA Interest Coverage

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

EBIT (Operating Income) = $133,050M

Depreciation and amortization = $11,698M

EBITDA = 133,050 + 11,698 = 144,748

Interest Expense = not separately disclosed

EBITDA = $144,748M; ratio cannot be completed

EBITDA can be built from the income statement and cash flow statement shown on this page, but interest expense is not broken out separately (see the Interest Coverage Ratio note above), so the full EBITDA-to-interest ratio cannot be calculated accurately here.

\[ \text{EBITDA Interest Coverage Ratio} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{EBIT}} \color{#a73193}{\text{DA}} }{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{Interest Expense}} } \]

EBITDA = Operating Income (EBIT) + Depreciation + Amortization.

Note

EBITDA is useful as a pre-interest, pre-tax operating performance measure, but it is not the same as cash flow because it does not account for capital expenditures, working-capital requirements, taxes, or principal repayments.

Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Repayments of term debt (financing activities) = $10,932M

Interest Expense = not separately disclosed

"Cash Available for Debt Service" = not a defined line item here

Cannot be calculated reliably

The cash flow statement shows actual debt repayments (not necessarily the same as scheduled "required" principal), and interest expense is not separately disclosed. Because both the numerator and part of the denominator rely on figures this page does not provide, DSCR cannot be calculated accurately from the available data.

\[ \text{DSCR} = \frac{ \text{Cash Available for Debt Service} }{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{Interest Expense}} + \color{#1b59df}{\text{Required Principal Repayments}} } \]
Cash Available for Debt Service (CADS) = Operating Cash Generation − Cash Needed to Maintain Operations

Cash Available for Debt Service (CADS) is the cash generated by the business that is available to meet its scheduled debt obligations, including interest and required principal repayments.

CADS is generally a calculated measure rather than a single line item that can be directly extracted from the financial statements. Its exact calculation depends on the lender's or credit agreement's definition.

Note

DSCR definitions differ across lenders and credit agreements — "cash available for debt service" may be defined differently from one lender to another.

Reading the result
  • DSCR > 1: available cash exceeds scheduled debt service under the selected definition.
  • DSCR = 1: available cash exactly covers scheduled debt service.
  • DSCR < 1: available cash is insufficient to cover scheduled debt service under the selected definition.

No fixed value (e.g. 1.20 or 1.25) is universally acceptable — required levels are typically set by the specific lender or credit agreement.

Profitability

⌄

Profitability ratios measure the company's ability to generate earnings from its sales, assets, and capital.

Gross Profit Margin

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Gross Margin = $195,201M

Total Net Sales (Revenue) = $416,161M

Gross Profit Margin = 195,201 ÷ 416,161 × 100
= 46.91%
\[ \text{Gross Profit Margin} = \frac{ \color{#16a34a}{\text{Gross Profit}} }{ \color{#16a34a}{\text{Revenue}} } \times 100\% \]
Meaning

Gross Profit Margin shows the percentage of revenue remaining after direct production costs, before operating expenses. It tells you how much of each dollar of revenue remains after paying the direct costs of producing or purchasing the goods or services sold (COGS).

Potential warning signals

Declining gross margins over time.

Operating Profit Margin

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Operating Income (EBIT) = $133,050M

Total Net Sales (Revenue) = $416,161M

Operating Profit Margin = 133,050 ÷ 416,161 × 100
= 31.97%

Interpretation: The 32.0% margin means the borrower generates about $0.32 of operating profit from each $1 of revenue before interest and tax. For a lender, the result should be considered alongside trend, industry norms, and the borrower’s cash-flow forecast because it may indicate weaker operating cash-generation capacity.

\[ \text{Operating Profit Margin} = \frac{ \color{#16a34a}{\text{EBIT}} }{ \color{#16a34a}{\text{Revenue}} } \times 100\% \]

EBIT: Earnings Before Interest and Taxes

EBIT = Net Income + Interest Expense + Income Tax Expense

Meaning

Profitability from core operations before interest and taxes.

Net Profit Margin

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Net Income = $112,010M

Total Net Sales (Revenue) = $416,161M

Net Profit Margin = 112,010 ÷ 416,161 × 100
= 26.92%
\[ \text{Net Profit Margin} = \frac{ \color{#16a34a}{\text{Net Income}} }{ \color{#16a34a}{\text{Revenue}} } \times 100\% \]
Meaning

Bottom-line profit as a percentage of revenue, after all expenses, interest, and taxes.

EBITDA Margin

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Operating Income (EBIT) = $133,050M

Depreciation and Amortization = $11,698M

Total Net Sales (Revenue) = $416,161M

EBITDA = Operating Income (EBIT) + Depreciation + Amortization
= 133,050 + 11,698
= 144,748

EBITDA Margin = 144,748 ÷ 416,161 × 100
= 34.78%
\[ \text{EBITDA Margin} = \frac{ \color{#16a34a}{\text{EBIT}} \color{#a73193}{\text{DA}} }{ \color{#16a34a}{\text{Revenue}} } \times 100\% \]

EBITDA = Operating Income (EBIT) + Depreciation + Amortization.

Meaning

Measures how much EBITDA the company generates from each dollar of sales before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization.

In other words, it shows the company's operating profitability before considering its financing costs, taxes, and non-cash depreciation and amortization expenses.

Credit interpretation

A higher EBITDA margin generally indicates stronger operating profitability and a larger earnings cushion before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. For lenders, EBITDA is often used when evaluating leverage and debt-service capacity.

Potential warning signals
  • Declining EBITDA margin over time
  • EBITDA growing more slowly than revenue
  • Large differences between EBITDA and operating cash flow
  • Strong EBITDA but weak cash generation
Industry comparison

EBITDA margins vary significantly by industry because businesses have different operating cost structures and capital intensity. Compare the ratio with the company's historical performance and similar companies.

Return on Assets (ROA)

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Net Income (FY2025) = $112,010M

Total Assets (FY2024) = $364,980M

Total Assets (FY2025) = $359,241M

Average Total Assets = (364,980 + 359,241) ÷ 2 = 362,110.5M
ROA = 112,010 ÷ 362,110.5 × 100
≈ 30.93%

For every $100 invested in assets, the company generates about $30.93 of net income.

\[ \text{ROA} = \frac{ \color{#16a34a}{\text{Net Income}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Average Total Assets}} } \times 100\% \]
Average Total Assets is the average of current and previous year total assets.
Meaning

ROA shows how efficiently a company uses its assets to generate profit. A 30.93% ROA means Apple generated approximately $0.31 of net income for every $1 of average assets during FY2025.

Financial Statement Sources

Net Income comes from the Income Statement, while current-year and previous-year Total Assets come from the Balance Sheet.

Return on Equity (ROE)

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Net Income = $112,010M

Shareholders' Equity — beginning = $56,950M

Shareholders' Equity — ending = $73,733M

Average Equity = (56,950 + 73,733) ÷ 2 = 65,341.5
ROE = 112,010 ÷ 65,341.5 × 100
= 171.42%

For every $100 of shareholders’ equity, the company generates about $171.42 of net income.

\[ \text{ROE} = \frac{ \color{#16a34a}{\text{Net Income}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Average Shareholders' Equity}} } \times 100\% \]
Meaning

Return generated on the owners' invested capital.

Potential warning signals

Unusually high ROE can sometimes result from a very small equity base or high financial leverage, rather than strong underlying performance.

For each ratio, compare trend over time and against industry peers - no single value is automatically favorable.

Efficiency / Activity

⌄

Efficiency ratios show how effectively a company manages its operating assets and liabilities.

Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)

⌄

ROCE measures how efficiently a company uses long-term capital invested in the business to generate operating profit.

ROCE

Apple FY2025 Example

EBIT (Operating Income) = $133,050M

Total Assets = $359,241M

Current Liabilities = $165,631M

Capital Employed = 359,241 − 165,631 = 193,610
ROCE = 133,050 ÷ 193,610 × 100
= 68.72%

Uses Apple's FY2025 ending Total Assets and Current Liabilities, consistent with the "Total Assets − Current Liabilities" definition of Capital Employed used on this page.

\[ \text{ROCE} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{EBIT}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Capital Employed}} } \times 100\% \]
Where

EBIT = Earnings Before Interest and Taxes, and commonly Capital Employed = Total Assets − Current Liabilities.

Conceptually

Capital Employed ≈ Shareholders' Equity + Long-Term Interest-Bearing Financing. Definitions can vary, so an analyst must apply the numerator and denominator consistently.

Why it matters
  • Assesses operating efficiency of long-term capital
  • Declining ROCE may indicate deteriorating performance
  • Compare with historical ROCE and industry peers

ROCE

Considers returns generated using both equity and long-term financing (the full capital base employed in the business).

ROE

Focuses on returns generated relative specifically to shareholders' equity.

Inventory Turnover

⌄

The inventory turnover ratio shows how many times a business sells and replaces its stock over a period.

\[ \text{Inventory Turnover} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{COGS}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Average Inventory}} } \]
Where

COGS = Cost of Goods Sold during the period, and Average Inventory is commonly calculated as:
(Beginning Inventory + Ending Inventory) ÷ 2 .

Conceptually

Inventory Turnover measures how many times a company sells and replaces its inventory during a period. It helps assess how efficiently inventory is being managed and converted into sales.

Why it matters
  • Helps assess how efficiently the company manages its inventory
  • Low or declining turnover may indicate slow-moving or obsolete inventory, overstocking, or weaker demand
  • Very high turnover may indicate strong inventory efficiency, but could also signal insufficient inventory levels
  • Compare with historical turnover and industry peers because normal inventory turnover varies significantly by industry
Apple FY2025 Example

Total Cost of Sales (COGS) = $ 220,960M

Beginning Inventory (FY2024 ending balance) = $ 7,286M

Ending Inventory (FY2025 ending balance) = $ 5,718M

Average Inventory = (7,286 + 5,718) ÷ 2 = 6,502
Inventory Turnover = 220,960 ÷ 6,502
≈ 33.98×

Interpretation: That means Apple sells and replaces its average inventory approximately 33.98 times during the year.

Note: Beginning and ending inventory are taken from Apple's comparative Balance Sheet. For FY2025, the FY2024 ending inventory balance of $7,286M serves as beginning inventory, while the FY2025 balance of $5,718M is ending inventory. Average inventory is used because COGS represents activity over the full fiscal year, while inventory is measured at a specific point in time.

Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO)

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Inventories (FY2025, ending) = $5,718M

Total Cost of Sales (COGS) = $220,960M

DIO ≈ 5,718 ÷ 220,960 × 365
≈ 9.45 days

Interpretation: Apple holds inventory for approximately 9.45 days, on average, before it is sold or used.

Uses ending inventory as an approximation for average inventory, for the same reason noted under Inventory Turnover.

DIO shows approximately how many days does inventory remain in the business before being sold or used?

\[ \text{Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO)} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{Average Inventory}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{COGS}} } \times 365 \]

Accounts Receivable Turnover

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Total Net Sales (Revenue) = $416,161M

Accounts receivable, net (FY2025, ending) = $39,777M

AR Turnover ≈ 416,161 ÷ 39,777
≈ 10.46×

Interpretation: Apple collects its average accounts receivable approximately 10.46 times per year.

Note:Apple's statements don't separately disclose "net credit sales," so Total Net Sales is used as an approximation. Only ending accounts receivable is shown on this page (no FY2024 beginning balance), so ending receivables is used in place of an average.

Receivables Turnover tells you how quickly a company collects money from customers who bought on credit.

\[ \text{Receivables Turnover} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{Net Credit Sales}} }{ \color{#a73193}{\text{Average Accounts Receivable}} } \]

A declining receivables turnover may indicate slower customer collections and increasing cash tied up in accounts receivable, which could weaken the borrower's liquidity and repayment capacity.

Days Sales Outstanding (DSO)

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Accounts receivable, net (FY2025, ending) = $39,777M

Total Net Sales (Revenue) = $416,161M

DSO ≈ 39,777 ÷ 416,161 × 365
≈ 34.89 days

Interpretation: This suggests relatively fast collection of receivables- equivalent to roughly 35 days of sales .

Note: Same approximations as Accounts Receivable Turnover above: Total Net Sales in place of net credit sales, and ending receivables in place of average receivables.

DSO shows approximately how many days it takes a company to collect its accounts receivable.

\[ \text{Days Sales Outstanding (DSO)} = \frac{ \color{#a73193}{\text{Average Accounts Receivable}} }{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{Net Credit Sales}} } \times 365 \]
Watch for

Increasing DSO can indicate slower collection, weaker customer payment behavior, looser credit terms, or changes in customer mix. A higher DSO should not automatically be treated as bad without examining payment terms and seasonality.

Accounts Payable Turnover

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Total Cost of Sales (COGS) = $220,960M

Accounts payable (FY2025, ending) = $69,860M

AP Turnover ≈ 220,960 ÷ 69,860
≈ 3.16×

Apple's statements do not disclose "purchases" separately, so COGS is used as the approximation the explanation on the right describes. Ending accounts payable is used because no FY2024 beginning balance is shown on this page.

\[ \text{Accounts Payable Turnover} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{Purchases}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Average Accounts Payable}} } \]

When purchases are unavailable, COGS ÷ Average Accounts Payable is sometimes used as an approximation, but it is not identical to using purchases.

Asset Turnover

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Total Net Sales (Revenue) = $416,161M

Total Assets (FY2025, ending) = $359,241M

Asset Turnover ≈ 416,161 ÷ 359,241
≈ 1.16×

Only the FY2025 ending Total Assets balance is shown on this page, so ending Total Assets is used here in place of average Total Assets.

\[ \text{Total Asset Turnover} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{Revenue}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Average Total Assets}} } \]

Equity Turnover (Sales-to-Equity)

⌄
Apple FY2025 Example

Total Net Sales (Revenue) = $416,161M

Shareholders' Equity — beginning = $56,950M

Shareholders' Equity — ending = $73,733M

Average Equity = (56,950 + 73,733) ÷ 2 = 65,341.5
Sales-to-Equity = 416,161 ÷ 65,341.5
= 6.37×

Beginning and ending equity are both shown in Apple's Statement of Shareholders' Equity, so a true average equity figure can be used here.

\[ \text{Equity Turnover} = \frac{ \color{#0c7973}{\text{Net Sales (or Revenue)}} }{ \color{#1b59df}{\text{Average Shareholders' Equity}} } \]

A high sales-to-equity ratio may reflect efficient use of owners' capital, but it can also arise from a small equity base or high leverage. It should always be interpreted together with leverage ratios.

4

Perform the Commercial Credit Assessment

⌄

Commercial credit risk assessment brings together quantitative analysis, qualitative assessment, and the proposed loan structure to determine whether a borrower is likely to repay its obligations and whether the level of risk is acceptable to the lender.

Financial ratios are important, but credit decisions should not be based on ratios alone. A commercial credit analyst also considers the borrower and its business, the purpose of financing, historical financial performance, cash flow, existing debt obligations, management quality, industry conditions, collateral, guarantees, loan structure, and key risks.

?

Central Credit Question

How will the borrower repay the loan, and what could prevent that repayment?

A. The 5 Cs of Credit — Conceptual Framework

⌄

The 5 Cs provide a useful framework for combining financial and qualitative information into a complete view of the borrower.

Character

“Can the borrower be trusted to meet its obligations?”

Character focuses on the borrower’s willingness to repay and the quality, credibility, and track record of management.

  • Management experience
  • Credit history
  • Previous repayment behavior
  • Reliability of financial information
  • Management reputation
  • Governance

Capacity

“Can the borrower generate enough cash to repay the debt?”

Capacity focuses on repayment ability and is normally one of the most important considerations in commercial lending.

  • Revenue and profitability
  • EBITDA / EBIT
  • Operating cash flow
  • DSCR
  • Interest coverage
  • Working capital
  • Existing debt obligations
  • Cash-flow stability

Capital

“How much financial support do the owners have invested?”

Capital considers the borrower’s equity base and the financial commitment of its owners. A stronger capital base can provide a cushion against unexpected losses.

  • Shareholders’ equity
  • Debt-to-equity
  • Leverage
  • Retained earnings
  • Owner equity contributions

Collateral

“What assets could support recovery if repayment fails?”

Collateral is generally a secondary source of repayment. Strong collateral should not automatically compensate for weak repayment capacity.

  • Accounts receivable
  • Inventory
  • Equipment
  • Real estate
  • Marketable securities
  • Other eligible assets

Analysts also consider market value, liquidity, quality, volatility, existing liens, recovery costs, and time required to realize the asset.

Conditions

“What external conditions could affect repayment?”

  • Industry outlook
  • Economic conditions
  • Interest rates
  • Competition
  • Regulation
  • Commodity prices where relevant
  • Geographic risks
  • Purpose of the loan

B. Commercial Credit Assessment Process

⌄

The 5 Cs provide a useful framework for understanding the major dimensions of creditworthiness. In practice, however, commercial credit analysis follows a broader process that combines business analysis, financial performance, repayment capacity, facility structure, downside testing, and professional judgment.

Central Credit Question

How will the borrower repay the credit, what could prevent repayment, and is the remaining risk acceptable to the lender?

A commercial credit assessment can be organized into the following analytical flow. The exact process and terminology may differ across lenders.

1. Borrower & Business Profile Business model · Management · Ownership
2. Purpose of the Credit Financing need · Facility purpose
3. Financial Performance & Trends Revenue · Profitability · Historical trends
4. Repayment Capacity Cash flow · DSCR · Debt service
5. Leverage & Liquidity Debt burden · Working capital · Financial cushion
6. Industry & Qualitative Risk Management · Industry · Concentration · External risk
7. Facility Structure Amount · Type · Term · Amortization
8. Collateral & Guarantees Security · Recovery · Secondary support
9. Covenants & Conditions Financial · Reporting · Protective conditions
10. Stress / Sensitivity Analysis Downside scenarios · Repayment resilience
11. Credit Strengths, Risks & Mitigants Synthesize · Balance · Assess residual risk
12. Final Credit Recommendation Approve · Conditional approval · Decline

Commercial credit analysis is a decision-making process, not simply a financial-ratio calculation.

1. Borrower & Business Profile

⌄

Before analyzing financial ratios, the analyst needs to understand how the business operates, how it generates cash, and what could disrupt that process.

Business Model

  • What products or services does the company provide?
  • How does the company generate revenue?
  • What are its major cost drivers?
  • Is the business seasonal or cyclical?

Customers & Suppliers

  • Who are the major customers?
  • Is revenue concentrated?
  • Who are the major suppliers?
  • Are critical inputs dependent on a small number of suppliers?

Ownership & Management

  • Who owns the company?
  • Who manages the business?
  • What is management's experience and track record?
  • Is there appropriate succession planning?

Competitive Position

  • What differentiates the business?
  • Who are its main competitors?
  • How strong is its market position?
  • What could threaten its business model?

Financial ratios show what is happening financially; understanding the business helps explain why it is happening.

2. Purpose of the Credit

⌄

The analyst needs to understand exactly why financing is requested and whether the proposed facility is appropriate for that need.

  • Working capital
  • Seasonal financing
  • Equipment purchase
  • Business expansion
  • Commercial real estate
  • Acquisition
  • Refinancing existing debt

Short-Term Need

Example: Seasonal or working-capital needs may be better matched with a revolving operating facility.

Long-Term Need

Example: Long-lived equipment may be financed with a term facility whose amortization reflects the asset's useful life and expected cash generation.

The purpose, term, and repayment structure of the facility should make economic sense together.

3. Financial Performance & Trend Analysis

⌄

Analysts normally examine several periods of financial information rather than relying on a single year. The objective is to determine whether performance is improving, stable, volatile, or deteriorating.

Revenue

  • Growing, stable, or declining?
  • Is growth sustainable?
  • Are sales volatile?
  • Is revenue concentrated?

Profitability

  • Gross margin
  • Operating margin
  • EBIT / EBITDA
  • Net profit margin

Earnings Quality

  • Are earnings generated by normal operations?
  • Are there large one-time gains or expenses?
  • Are margins stable?
  • Do earnings translate into cash flow?

Trend

  • Historical direction
  • Volatility
  • Peer / industry comparison
  • Relationship between growth and debt

Trend analysis is often more informative than a single-period ratio.

4. Repayment Capacity

⌄

“Where will the cash come from to repay principal and interest?”

Repayment capacity is generally one of the most important parts of commercial credit analysis. A borrower should normally be able to service debt through sustainable cash generated by the business.

Primary Source of Repayment

Normally cash generated through business operations.

  • Operating cash flow
  • Recurring business income
  • Cash available for debt service
  • Contracted cash flows where applicable

Secondary Source of Repayment

Support available if normal repayment fails.

  • Collateral realization
  • Guarantees
  • Asset sales
  • Other recovery sources
Business Operations Generate revenue
Cash Generation Cash becomes available
Debt Service Principal + interest

Cash Flow

  • Operating cash flow
  • Working-capital requirements
  • Capital expenditures
  • Owner distributions

Coverage

  • DSCR
  • Interest coverage
  • Fixed-charge coverage where applicable
  • Debt maturity requirements

Strong collateral should not automatically compensate for inadequate repayment capacity.

5. Leverage & Liquidity

⌄

Repayment capacity should be considered together with the borrower's balance-sheet strength. Leverage shows the extent of financial obligations, while liquidity indicates the borrower's ability to absorb short-term pressure.

Leverage

Measures how heavily the business relies on borrowed funds and other creditor financing.

  • Debt-to-equity
  • Debt-to-assets
  • Total liabilities-to-equity
  • Debt growth
  • Debt maturity profile

Liquidity

Measures the ability of the borrower to meet near-term obligations and manage working-capital requirements.

  • Current ratio
  • Quick ratio
  • Working capital
  • Cash reserves
  • Availability under operating facilities

High leverage reduces financial flexibility, while weak liquidity can create repayment pressure even when a business remains profitable.

6. Industry & Qualitative Risk Assessment

⌄

Not every material credit risk appears directly in the financial statements. Qualitative analysis helps identify factors that may affect the borrower's future financial performance and repayment ability.

Management Risk

  • Experience
  • Track record
  • Financial discipline
  • Governance
  • Succession planning

Industry Risk

  • Industry growth or decline
  • Cyclicality
  • Competition
  • Barriers to entry
  • Technology disruption

Customer Concentration

Dependence on one or a few major customers can materially affect revenue and cash flow if a relationship is lost.

Supplier Concentration

Dependence on critical suppliers can disrupt production, increase costs, or reduce margins.

Economic Risk

  • Interest rates
  • Inflation
  • Economic slowdown
  • Exchange rates where relevant
  • Commodity prices where relevant

Regulatory Risk

Legal or regulatory changes may affect costs, revenue, profitability, or the company's ability to operate.

7. Facility Structure

⌄

Credit risk depends not only on the borrower but also on how the proposed financing is structured.

Facility

  • Facility amount
  • Facility type
  • Currency where relevant
  • Committed or demand facility

Repayment Structure

  • Loan term
  • Amortization period
  • Principal repayment schedule
  • Balloon payment where applicable

Pricing

  • Interest rate
  • Fixed or floating rate
  • Fees where applicable

A well-structured facility attempts to match repayment requirements with the borrower's expected cash generation, financing purpose, and risk profile.

8. Collateral & Guarantees

⌄

Collateral and guarantees provide secondary support if the borrower cannot repay through normal business cash flow. They can reduce potential loss after default and therefore relate closely to Loss Given Default (LGD).

Default Borrower cannot repay
Recovery Collateral / guarantees
LGD Recovery affects lender loss

Collateral analysis may consider:

  • Type of collateral
  • Market and appraised value
  • Liquidity
  • Advance rate
  • Existing liens
  • Value volatility
  • Legal enforceability
  • Recovery costs
  • Time required to realize the asset

Guarantees may provide additional support, but their value depends on the guarantor's financial capacity and legal enforceability.

Collateral is normally a secondary source of repayment; sustainable business cash flow remains the primary source.

9. Covenants & Conditions

⌄

Covenants and approval conditions can help control risk, maintain financial discipline, and provide early warning if the borrower's risk profile deteriorates.

Financial Covenants

  • Minimum DSCR
  • Maximum leverage
  • Minimum working capital
  • Minimum tangible net worth

Non-Financial Covenants

  • Regular financial reporting
  • Maintain appropriate insurance
  • Restrictions on additional borrowing
  • Restrictions on major asset sales

Conditions of Approval

  • Equity injection before funding
  • Required collateral documentation
  • Guarantees
  • Updated appraisal
  • Repayment of specified existing debt
  • Other lender-specific conditions

Covenants help monitor and control risk; they do not eliminate the underlying credit risk.

10. Stress / Sensitivity Analysis

⌄

“What happens to repayment capacity if conditions become worse than expected?”

Stress testing examines whether the borrower can continue to service debt under reasonable downside assumptions.

Revenue Stress

  • Revenue declines
  • A major customer is lost
  • Sales growth slows

Margin Stress

  • Gross margin declines
  • Input costs increase
  • Operating expenses rise

Liquidity Stress

  • Receivable collection slows
  • Inventory increases
  • Working-capital requirements rise

Financing Stress

  • Interest rates increase
  • Debt refinancing becomes more difficult
  • Debt-service requirements increase
Base Case Expected performance
Downside Case Adverse assumptions
Reassess DSCR · Liquidity · Leverage

A borrower that can repay only under optimistic assumptions represents greater credit risk than one with sufficient financial cushion to absorb reasonable downside scenarios.

11. Credit Strengths, Risks & Mitigants

⌄

At this stage, the analyst brings together the findings from the entire assessment. The objective is not simply to list positive and negative factors, but to determine the borrower's overall credit position and residual risk.

Credit Strengths

  • Strong and stable operating cash flow
  • Consistent profitability
  • Moderate leverage
  • Strong liquidity
  • Experienced management
  • Diversified customer base
  • Strong equity support

Key Risks

  • High leverage
  • Weak liquidity
  • Declining margins
  • Customer concentration
  • Volatile cash flow
  • Industry cyclicality
  • Management succession risk

Risk Mitigants

  • Lower facility amount
  • Additional owner equity
  • Conservative amortization
  • Financial covenants
  • Collateral
  • Guarantees
  • Additional reporting requirements

Examples: Connecting Risks with Mitigants

Identified Risk Possible Mitigant
High leverage Lower facility amount / additional owner equity
Weak liquidity Liquidity requirements / working-capital controls
Customer concentration Long-term contracts / diversification / conservative assumptions
Volatile cash flow Greater cash-flow cushion / stronger DSCR requirement
Weak collateral coverage Lower advance rate / additional security
Increasing debt Maximum leverage covenant
Industry cyclicality Conservative structure / additional liquidity
Management succession risk Formal succession plan / additional support where appropriate

A mitigant reduces a risk; it does not necessarily eliminate it. The analyst must determine whether the remaining or residual risk is acceptable.

12. Final Credit Recommendation

⌄

The final recommendation synthesizes the complete analysis. A decision should not be based on one ratio, one piece of collateral, or one positive or negative factor.

Approve

Repayment capacity and overall credit risk are acceptable under the proposed facility structure.

Approve with Conditions

Financing may be acceptable if specific conditions or risk mitigants are implemented.

  • Lower facility amount
  • Additional equity
  • Additional collateral
  • Guarantee
  • Financial covenant
  • Additional reporting

Decline

Repayment capacity or overall credit risk remains unacceptable even after reasonable structural changes or mitigants are considered.

Purpose

Facility

Conditions / Covenants

Relevant requirements attached to the recommendation

Final Decision Logic

Can They Repay? Cash flow · DSCR · Financial capacity
What Could Go Wrong? Business · Industry · Financial risks
How Is Risk Controlled? Structure · Covenants · Collateral
Is Residual Risk Acceptable? Final credit judgment
