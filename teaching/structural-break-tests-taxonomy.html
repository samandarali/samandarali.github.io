A plain-language guide

Structural Break Tests

A structural break is a change in the pattern that generated a time series or regression relationship. This guide helps you match the research question to a suitable test or model.

What is a structural break test?

It asks whether the process behind the data stayed stable over time.

A structural break happens when one or more features of a data-generating process change. For example, the average level, trend, regression slopes, volatility, or dynamic relationships may differ after a point in time.

A test compares a stable-process explanation with one that allows a change. Some tests use a date chosen in advance; others search the data for a likely date. The appropriate method depends on what changed, what is known about timing, and the data structure.

Break detection is not causal proof

A detected break says the statistical pattern changed. By itself, it does not prove that a particular policy, law, or crisis caused the change. Causal attribution needs a design and assumptions that address other events, trends, and confounders.

A quick guide

Choose by your question

Begin with timing, then decide whether you are testing stability or modelling changing regimes.

01 · DATE SET IN ADVANCE

Do I have a specific break date?

Use a known-date test when an external event gives you a date before looking for one in the outcome data.

 02 · DATE NOT KNOWN

Should the data locate one break?

Use an unknown-date test when timing is uncertain. Searching across dates changes the test’s critical values.

 03 · SEVERAL BREAKS

Could there be multiple regimes?

Use multiple-break methods when the process may change more than once and you need to estimate the number and dates.
The taxonomy

Tests and models by setting

Open each item for a short explanation, when it fits, and a key limitation.

A

One break at a known date

The date comes from external information or a pre-specified research design.

Chow test
Known dateRegression coefficients

What it does: Compares a pooled regression with separate regressions before and after the specified date. The null is that the tested coefficients are equal across the two periods.

Use it when: You have a single, defensible date specified independently of the outcome data and a regression model.

Watch out: The classic Chow test assumes equal error variance across regimes. If variance may differ, use a heteroskedasticity-robust Wald test. If you selected the date by searching the data, use an unknown-date procedure instead.

Robust Wald test for a known break
Known dateUnequal variance allowed

What it does: Tests whether specified regression parameters differ across periods, using standard errors robust to heteroskedasticity.

Use it when: The date is known in advance and unequal error variance is plausible.

Watch out: Robust standard errors do not fix a misspecified model, serial correlation, or a date chosen after inspecting the data. Choose a covariance estimator that fits the time-series errors.

MZ test (mean and variance jointly)
Known dateMean and variance

What it does: In the Maasoumi–Zaman–Ahmed framework, the MZ likelihood-ratio statistic tests joint homogeneity of regression coefficients and error variance across regimes.

Use it when: You want to test a joint change in the conditional mean relationship and variance at a specified date.

Watch out: State the model and version you are using. MZ is a particular test, not a generic label for every test of mean and variance shifts.

B

One break at an unknown date

The date is estimated by searching a range of candidate dates.

Sup-Wald / sup-LR / sup-LM (QLR or sup-F)
Unknown dateOne break

What it does: Computes a Wald, likelihood-ratio, or Lagrange-multiplier statistic at each candidate date, then uses the largest value. In a linear regression, this is often described as a sup-F or Quandt likelihood-ratio (QLR) test.

Use it when: You want a formal test for one break but do not know its date.

Watch out: The date is unidentified when there is no break, so ordinary single-date critical values do not apply. Use the test’s nonstandard critical values, allow a minimum segment length, and account for heteroskedasticity or serial correlation as appropriate.

CUSUM and CUSUM of squares
Stability diagnosticsRecursive residuals

What they do: CUSUM tracks cumulative recursive residuals to assess regression-parameter stability. CUSUM-SQ tracks cumulative squared recursive residuals and is particularly sensitive to variance instability.

Use them when: You want a visual and formal diagnostic for instability over time, especially in a regression estimated recursively.

Watch out: They do not usually report a precise break date, and their power depends on how the parameters change. CUSUM is not limited to intercept or mean changes.

Sup-MZ
Unknown dateMean and variance

What it does: Searches candidate dates using an MZ-type statistic, allowing a joint change in regression coefficients and error variance.

Use it when: You suspect a break may affect both the conditional mean relationship and volatility.

Watch out: Check the method’s assumptions and finite-sample guidance. It is a specialized alternative, not automatically the best choice for every regression.

Zivot–Andrews test
Unit rootOne unknown break

What it does: Tests a unit-root null against a trend-stationary alternative that allows one break in the intercept, trend, or both, depending on the specification.

Use it when: The research question is whether a single time series has a unit root when a one-time structural break may be present.

Watch out: This is a unit-root test, not a general regression-break test. The break is allowed under the alternative in the standard setup; this affects interpretation.

C

Several unknown breaks

Estimate multiple break dates and, in some procedures, determine how many are supported.

Bai–Perron framework
Multiple breaksRegression

What it does: Estimates break dates by globally minimizing the sum of squared residuals across regimes, using dynamic programming. Testing and information criteria can help choose the number of breaks.

Use it when: You need to identify several changes in regression relationships over a sufficiently long series.

Watch out: Results depend on the minimum regime length, model specification, and assumptions about errors. The often-cited (O(T^2)) refers to the order of least-squares computations for the algorithm, not a universal wall-clock runtime guarantee.

Panel structural-break methods
Panel dataGroup or common breaks

What they do: Extend break analysis to observations across units and time. Methods differ in whether they allow common break dates, unit-specific dates, or changing coefficients across groups.

Examples: Stata commands such as xtbreak implement panel break-testing approaches. Select the method based on whether breaks are expected to be common or heterogeneous across units.

Watch out: Panel methods are not interchangeable. Check the command’s exact model, supported break pattern, and inference assumptions.

D

Unit roots and cointegration with breaks

Use these only when the question concerns nonstationary series or a long-run relationship.

Gregory–Hansen test
CointegrationOne unknown break

What it does: Tests for cointegration while allowing one unknown regime shift in the long-run relationship. Specifications can allow a level shift and, in some versions, a shift in slopes.

Use it when: The variables are nonstationary and you want to test whether a cointegrating relationship exists despite one possible break.

Watch out: Select the model form to match the type of shift you consider plausible. It is not a general test for any time-series break.

Hatemi-J and Maki tests
CointegrationMultiple breaks

What they do: Cointegration-test extensions that permit more than one unknown break, with the number and placement allowed depending on the particular method.

Use them when: A long-run relationship is the focus and one break may be too restrictive.

Watch out: Check the exact variant and its assumptions; the names cover different specifications, and extra break flexibility requires enough data.

E

Flexible ways to represent change

These approaches search for or model changing patterns; they are not all conventional break tests.

GETS / indicator saturation
Automated searchOutliers and shifts

What it does: General-to-specific methods add and test indicator variables. Impulse indicators can flag unusual observations, while step or trend indicators can represent shifts or changing trends.

Use it when: You want a systematic search for outliers, level shifts, or trend changes in a specified model.

Watch out: Results depend on the search procedure, selection settings, and model. Treat selected dates as evidence to investigate, not automatic causal explanations.

Threshold and Markov-switching models
Regime modelsSwitching dynamics

What they do: Threshold models let parameters change when an observed variable crosses a threshold. Markov-switching models let an unobserved state govern probabilistic transitions between regimes.

Use them when: You want to describe recurring or state-dependent regimes rather than test for one isolated break.

Watch out: Regime labels and transition probabilities need careful interpretation; model complexity can be hard to identify in short samples.

Time-varying parameter models
Gradual changeState space

What they do: Let parameters evolve over time, often in a state-space model estimated with a Kalman filter.

Use them when: Change is expected to be gradual or continuous rather than a sudden jump at one date.

Watch out: They describe evolving parameters rather than identify a discrete break. Results depend on how parameter evolution and noise are specified.

Before you run a test

A practical workflow

These checks make results easier to interpret and less likely to reflect modelling choices.

Define the change

Specify whether you expect a shift in level, trend, slope, variance, or a long-run relationship.

Choose the timing setup

Decide whether the date is externally known, must be estimated, or there may be several dates.

Check the data and model

Consider frequency, sample length, seasonality, autocorrelation, outliers, and transformations.

Report uncertainty

State the null, specification, search range, critical values, estimated dates, and sensitivity checks.

Multiple searching raises false positives

Trying many dates, outcomes, model forms, or break counts and reporting only the strongest result makes evidence look stronger than it is. Pre-specify what you can, use the appropriate multiple-search inference, and report sensitivity analyses.

Small samples limit break detection

Each regime needs enough observations to estimate its parameters. Annual data can be especially limiting because even a long calendar span may contain few observations. No test can reliably recover several breaks from a short series.
